# Named-Diet Evidence Tables — Home Chef Companion Kit v1.0.0

**Reference appendix.** Consulted only when the user asks for the evidence behind a claim. Evidence grades: **A** = strong RCT / consistent meta-analysis · **B** = consistent observational · **C** = preliminary, small, or short-term · **D** = contested or insufficient. Contradictions are flagged, not resolved.

---

## 1. Mediterranean

The pattern: vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole grains, nuts, olive oil as the primary added fat, fish and seafood, herbs and spices. Moderate dairy (mostly yogurt and cheese), poultry, eggs. Limited red meat, sweets, and highly processed foods. Based on 1960s Crete/southern Italy/Greece eating patterns.

| Claim | Grade | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|---|
| Fewer major cardiovascular events (heart attack, stroke, CV death) in high-risk adults | **A** (one large RCT + observational) | PREDIMED (n=7,447, median ~4.8 years): roughly 30% fewer major events in the Mediterranean + extra-virgin olive oil arm (HR 0.70; 95% CI 0.53–0.91) and the Mediterranean + nuts arm (HR 0.70; 0.53–0.94) vs. a low-fat control. No calorie restriction, no added exercise. Cite the 2018 NEJM republication — the 2013 paper was retracted over randomization-protocol deviations and re-analyzed. A 2026 VA evidence brief notes PREDIMED is the only large RCT of a Mediterranean pattern on clinical outcomes; three smaller secondary-prevention RCTs show fewer new heart attacks but no reduction in CV death or stroke. |
| Lower diabetes incidence | **A–** | PREDIMED: reduced incident diabetes in the olive-oil arm (HR 0.60; 0.43–0.85) only — not the nuts arm. |
| Better cardiometabolic risk factors | **A–** | 2025 meta-analysis of 26 RCTs in overweight/obese adults (n=10,352): reduced BMI, waist, triglycerides, fatty-liver index; GRADE quality very low for most outcomes; 85% of trials had bias concerns (PMID 39783962). |
| Lower cancer / all-cause mortality | **B** | Consistent observational cohorts; PREDIMED showed reduced breast-cancer incidence in the olive-oil arm (women only). No RCT evidence of reduced cancer recurrence or mortality. |
| Alignment with major guidance | Authority position | American Heart Association scientific statement (*Circulation*, 2023; Gardner et al.): Mediterranean scored **89/100** for alignment with the AHA's 2021 dietary guidance — Tier 1 (best-aligned group, alongside DASH and pescatarian). Quote as "one organization's assessment against its own criteria," not a verdict. |

**Bottom line:** the only pattern here with a large trial showing fewer hard heart outcomes. Present as "reduces risk," never "prevents heart disease."

## 2. Ketogenic

The pattern: very-low-carbohydrate (National Lipid Association: ≤50 g/day or ≤10% of calories; AHA: <10% of calories), high-fat (>70% of calories per NLA), moderate protein. Meat, fish, eggs, full-fat dairy, butter, oils, non-starchy vegetables, nuts, seeds. Out: all grains, legumes, starchy vegetables, most fruit, added sugars. Originally a 1920s medical therapy for epilepsy; weight-loss/diabetes use came later. (1920s origin widely documented; primary citation not re-verified.)

| Claim | Grade | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|---|
| Greater weight loss than other diets | **C–D (contested, time-dependent)** | Cochrane review of low-carb vs. balanced-carb weight-reducing diets: low-carb "probably" makes little to no difference — about 1 kg short-term (3–8.5 months), under 1 kg at 1–2 years. The NLA 2019 statement: low/very-low-carb diets "are not superior to other dietary approaches for weight loss." A 2024 network meta-analysis (17 RCTs, n=5,802) ranked keto highest for weight/BMI/waist — a genuine inconsistency across reviews (method- and definition-dependent). Any advantage is small and may fade. |
| Better glycemia in type 2 diabetes | **C (short-term), D long-term** | Meta-analysis of 10 RCTs (n=1,376): low-to-moderate carb lowered HbA1c ~0.34% more than higher-carb diets in year 1; no difference at ≥1 year; no difference in weight, LDL, quality of life, or dropout. Some trials showed reduced diabetes medication use. |
| Lipid effects | **C–D** | Consistent triglyceride reduction and HDL increase vs. low-fat comparators; **LDL effects mixed — some trials show increases.** A 2024 UK Biobank cohort (Iatan et al., PMC11287001) linked a low-carb/high-fat pattern to higher LDL/apoB and more cardiovascular events — observational, not a trial. Minimal data beyond 2 years. |
| Alignment with major guidance | Authority position | AHA 2023 statement: keto scored **31/100 — worst of all 10 patterns evaluated, Tier 4**. Reasons: restricts whole fruit/legumes/whole grains, high saturated fat, lost phytochemicals, deficiency risk. |

**Bottom line:** short-term appetite control and triglyceride/HbA1c improvements are reasonably supported; weight-loss superiority and any long-term benefit are contested; LDL can rise in some people; long-term data are minimal. Never promise weight loss or cholesterol improvement.

## 3. Paleo / Primal

The pattern: meat, fish, eggs, vegetables, fruits, nuts, seeds. Out: all grains, all legumes (including peanuts), dairy, refined sugar, processed foods, industrial seed oils. **Primal** (Mark Sisson's variant) is looser: raw/fermented dairy is a "gray area," nightshades aren't specially restricted, dark chocolate and moderate alcohol fit the modern 80/20 framing. The shared rationale — eating like pre-agricultural hunter-gatherers — is an advocacy narrative, not a trial-tested hypothesis; note the internal tension that paleo bans dairy while primal embraces it.

| Claim | Grade | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|---|
| Better metabolic-syndrome components | **C** | Cochrane-methodology systematic review (*AJCN* 2015; PMC4588744): 4 RCTs, n=159 — moderate-quality evidence of greater *short-term* improvements in each metabolic-syndrome component vs. guideline-based control diets. All trials short; authors called for more research. |
| Better weight / lipids / blood pressure / glucose | **C** | 2026 GRADE-assessed meta-analysis (19 RCTs + 12 cohorts; PMID 41129328): RCTs showed small improvements in fasting insulin, total cholesterol, LDL, triglycerides, weight, BMI, and diastolic BP vs. controls — small, short trials. Cohorts: high adherers had 10% lower all-cause mortality, 10% lower cancer mortality, 16% lower CHD incidence — observational, confounded by healthy-user effects. |
| Primal-specific evidence | **D** | No RCTs of "Primal" as defined by Sisson were found; its evidence base is borrowed from paleo/low-carb trials. The paleo-vs-primal differences (dairy, nightshades, chocolate, alcohol) are proponent preference, not evidence-based. |
| Alignment with major guidance | Authority position | AHA 2023 statement: paleo scored **53/100, Tier 4** — exclusion of legumes/whole grains plus higher animal/saturated fat. |

**Bottom line:** small, short-term trials show metabolic-marker improvements; no hard-outcome trials; long-term data lacking. Dairy is the key paleo-vs-primal recipe fork.

## 4. Carnivore

The pattern: animal foods only; plant foods excluded entirely. Strictest ("Lion Diet"): beef, salt, water. Looser versions include eggs, fish, dairy, organ meats. There is no standardized definition.

| Claim | Grade | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|---|
| Health improvements (weight, GI, autoimmune symptoms) | **D** | No RCTs exist. Best available: a 2026 PRISMA-scoping review (*Nutrients* 18(2):348; PMID 41599961) of **9 human studies** (5 case reports/series, 2 social-media surveys, 1 exploratory study, 1 modeling study) — self-reported weight loss, satiety, some metabolic/inflammatory marker improvements; evidence quality "very limited." A 2021 survey (n=2,029, self-selected) reported high satisfaction — but 27% reported worsening lipid profiles. Severe selection bias: these are enthusiasts, not trials. |
| Raises LDL/total cholesterol | **C (small studies)** | Consistent direction: LDL-C and total cholesterol rise. Modeling (Goedecke et al. 2024) shows above-requirement intakes of vitamins A/B2/B6/B12, zinc, selenium, sodium, phosphorus. |
| Causes nutrient deficiencies | **C (modeling/small)** | Modeling + small studies flag shortfalls: vitamin C, vitamin D, calcium, magnesium, iodine, folate, fiber, potassium. Stricter variants (no dairy/seafood) amplify risk. |
| Processed-meat cancer risk | **B (observational)** | IARC (2015): processed meat = Group 1 (carcinogenic), red meat = Group 2A (probably carcinogenic); +18% colorectal-cancer risk per 50 g/day processed meat. Present both halves: individual risk small, population impact meaningful. |
| Overall verdict | Authority position | The 2026 scoping review's conclusion: "may offer short-term health benefits but carries substantial risks of nutrient deficiencies, reduced intake of health-promoting phytochemicals, and the development of cardiovascular disease. At this time, long-term adherence to a CD cannot be recommended." |

**Bottom line:** the weakest evidence base of the four — anecdotes and self-reports only. The companion may cook to it as a stated preference but must not present any health claim as evidence-backed, and should note the deficiency and LDL concerns neutrally.

## 5. Where the diets contradict each other

| Flashpoint | Mediterranean | Keto | Paleo / Primal | Carnivore |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Saturated fat | Replace with unsaturated (olive oil); limit butter/red meat | Saturated fat embraced; butter, coconut oil, tallow are staples | Saturated fat from whole-food animal sources accepted; industrial seed oils rejected | Saturated animal fat is the energy base |
| Grains | Foundation (whole grains) | All excluded | All excluded (both) | All excluded |
| Legumes | Emphasized | Excluded | Excluded (primal allows beans in moderation in evolved versions) | Excluded |
| Dairy | Moderate, mostly fermented | Embraced (full-fat) | Paleo: excluded. Primal: raw/fermented allowed | Often included |
| Fruit | Emphasized | Berries, small amounts | Emphasized | Excluded |
| Fiber | High | Often low; framed as optional | Moderate–high via vegetables | Near zero; framed as unnecessary |
| Red/processed meat | Limited | Staple | Staple (processed discouraged) | Foundation |

### How to present contradictions neutrally

1. **Attribute, don't adjudicate.** Describe the mechanism of the disagreement: "Keto skips legumes to protect a tight carb budget; Mediterranean centers them for fiber and plant protein." Never declare a winner.
2. **Grade, don't flatten.** Mediterranean's cardiometabolic claims rest on a large trial; carnivore's rest on surveys. Neutrality means not hiding the asymmetry, not pretending all claims are equal.
3. **Saturated fat is the flashpoint.** Present the AHA/guideline position as an authority position and the low-carb position as a dispute — never resolved medically here. Don't present fringe arguments ("lean mass hyper-responder," "LDL is irrelevant") at all.
4. **Legumes/grains.** Present the trade-off factually: they supply fiber, potassium, magnesium, and phytochemicals (guideline view) and cost carbohydrate budget (keto/carnivore view). Teach preparation techniques without taking a side.
5. **Fiber.** Guidelines set fiber targets; some low-carb communities call fiber non-essential. Present as: "guidelines recommend it; some low-carb communities disagree; long-term data on zero-fiber diets do not exist." Never echo "fiber is unnecessary" as fact.
6. **Processed meat.** State the IARC classification (2015) factually when users cook carnivore/keto with bacon or sausage — a material safety fact, not an opinion.
7. **No "healthiest diet" verdicts.** The companion ranks nothing.

## 6. Vegetarian and vegan — the AND 2025 anchor

Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, *Vegetarian Dietary Patterns for Adults* position paper (approved January 2025, in effect through December 31, 2032): "It is the position of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics that, in adults, **appropriately planned** vegetarian and vegan dietary patterns can be nutritionally adequate and can offer long-term health benefits such as improving several health outcomes associated with cardiometabolic diseases." Vegetarian patterns exclude meat, poultry, and seafood; vegan patterns exclude all foods of animal origin.

**Scope limit (verified from the primary text): adults ≥18 who are not pregnant or lactating.** The companion refers out for under-18s, pregnancy, and lactation. **Verify correction applied:** the companion does not name zinc, omega-3s, or protein as AND-2025-designated nutrients of concern — those were not re-confirmed from the 2025 text. (The 2016 predecessor paper named B12, iron, zinc, calcium, vitamin D, and omega-3s; the 2025 paper's confirmed nutrient discussion covers B12, D, iron/ferritin, iodine, choline, and calcium/bone health.)

Nutrient notes worth knowing (general information, never personal advice, never doses): B12 occurs naturally only in animal products — vegan cooks typically rely on fortified foods or supplements; fortified nutritional yeast provides B12 only if the label says so. Iron from plants is non-heme and less bioavailable; pairing with vitamin C (citrus, bell peppers, tomatoes) improves absorption. Current dietetic guidance: variety across the day covers amino acids — same-meal "protein combining" is outdated advice.

## 7. WW — what the companion can cite

WW's Points formula is proprietary and unpublished; officially, protein, fiber, and unsaturated fat push Points down while saturated fat and added sugar push them up. 350+ ZeroPoint foods (variants for diabetes and menopause programs exist). Members get a personalized daily budget plus weekly points. The companion estimates Points per serving only, always labeled "unofficial estimate — confirm in the WW app," using the user's stated budget — never calculating one.
