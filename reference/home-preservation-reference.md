## 1. Blanching

### Why (NCHFP)

Blanching (scalding vegetables in boiling water or steam for a short time) is a **must for almost all vegetables to be frozen**. It stops enzyme actions that cause loss of flavor, color, texture, and nutrients — and **enzyme action continues even in freezer storage**. Secondary benefits: cleanses the surface, brightens color, helps retain vitamins, wilts vegetables for easier packing.

**The under-blanching trap — NCHFP's exact words:** *"Underblanching stimulates the activity of enzymes and is worse than no blanching. Overblanching causes loss of flavor, color, vitamins and minerals."* Blanch times are exact, never "roughly."

### Method (NCHFP — verbatim rules, not times)

- **Water blanching (preferred for home freezing):** **1 gallon of boiling water per pound** of prepared vegetables. Use a blancher with a basket and cover (or a wire basket in a large pot with a lid). Lower the basket into vigorously boiling water and cover. **The water should return to boiling within 1 minute** — if it doesn't, you're blanching too much at once. **Start counting blanching time as soon as the water returns to a boil.** Keep the heat high the whole time.
- **Steam blanching:** recommended for only a few vegetables (broccoli, pumpkin, sweet potatoes, winter squash are satisfactory both ways). **Steam blanching takes about 1½ times longer than water blanching.** Tight-lidded pot, basket holding food ≥3 inches above the bottom, 1–2 inches of water, single layer; start counting when the lid is on.
- **Cooling:** cool vegetables promptly after blanching before packaging. (The research notes NCHFP's ice-water cooling wording was not captured verbatim this pass — see §6, Verification gaps — so the kit points outward rather than hardcoding it. Standard practice: ice-water bath, then drain well.)

### Blanching times — point to the table, don't reprint it

The full per-vegetable table (~35 vegetables, water-blanching times in minutes) lives at:

- **NCHFP, "Blanching Times":** https://nchfp.uga.edu/how/freeze/freeze-general-information/blanching-times/
- Method page: https://nchfp.uga.edu/how/freeze/freeze-general-information/blanching-vegetables/

The companion directs users to that table for each specific vegetable and does not recite it from memory. (Deliberately not duplicated here: per-vegetable times are the NCHFP page's job, and a stale copy in the kit is worse than a pointer to the current one.)

### Vegetables that don't need blanching — a verification gap

A widely circulated chart (attributed to NCHFP + Ball Blue Book) lists **onions, peppers, and tomatoes** as "blanching not required" — but NCHFP's own times table lists blanching times for onions and sweet peppers, contradicting it. The companion treats "no blanching needed for onions/peppers/tomatoes" as **unverified** and defers to NCHFP's per-vegetable pages.

## 2. Canning — full procedure

All rules below are from NCHFP's "How to Use Boiling Water and Pressure Canners" (adapted from the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning).

### 2.1 The pH 4.6 line

- **pH ≤ 4.6 → boiling-water bath.** Foods: fruits, properly pickled vegetables, jams/jellies, fruit butters, sauerkraut, **tomatoes and figs only if acidified** (some varieties test above 4.6 — acidify with lemon juice or citric acid).
- **pH above 4.6 → pressure canner required.** Foods: all fresh vegetables, meats, poultry, seafood, soups, stocks, dairy — and any mixture containing them (salsa, meat spaghetti sauce, relishes are low-acid unless a tested recipe proves uniform pH ≤ 4.6).
- Why: boiling water (212°F at sea level) kills molds, yeasts, and most bacteria in high-acid food because *C. botulinum* **cannot grow** at pH ≤ 4.6 — but its **spores survive boiling**. In a sealed, low-acid, airless jar the spores germinate and produce odorless, colorless, tasteless toxin. Pressure canning reaches **240°F at 10.5 psi gauge pressure**, the temperature needed to destroy the spores.

### 2.2 Water-bath procedure (NCHFP verbatim steps)

1. Rack in canner; fill about half with water — target **1–2 inches over jar tops**.
2. Preheat water to **140°F for raw-pack**, **180°F for hot-pack**.
3. Load jars upright with a jar lifter — keep upright; tilting spills food onto the sealing surface.
4. Top up with hot water so the level is **at least 1 inch above jar tops** (2 inches if the process time is over 30 minutes).
5. High heat, lid on, bring to a **vigorous boil**.
6. **Start the timer only when the water is boiling**; keep a full boil for the whole process.
7. **If the water stops boiling at any point: restart the timing from the beginning.** Quality may suffer, but safety requires it.
8. Time up: heat off, lid off, **wait 5 minutes** before lifting jars.
9. Set jars on a towel with at least 1 inch between them; **cool undisturbed at room temperature for 12–24 hours**. **Do not retighten bands** while hot (it cuts the gasket and breaks the seal).

Jars/lids: standard Mason-type jars with **two-piece self-sealing lids**; **new flat lid every use** (never reuse flat lids); fingertip-tight. Mayo/commercial jars are **not recommended for pressure canning**; acceptable for acid foods in a water bath with new two-piece lids, expecting more seal failures.

### 2.3 Pressure-canner procedure (NCHFP verbatim rules)

**Venting — the 10 minutes that matter.** Trapped air lowers the temperature reached at a given pressure → under-processing. USDA requires **all** pressure canners to be vented 10 minutes before pressurizing: vent port uncovered (or petcock open), lid locked, heat on high until steam flows freely in a visible funnel shape; **time 10 minutes of continuous steam**; then close the petcock or set the weight.

**Processing:**
- **Start timing only when** the dial reaches the recommended pressure or the weight begins to jiggle/rock per the manufacturer's description. Regulate heat for steady pressure at or slightly above target.
- **If pressure drops below the recommended amount at any time: bring it back to pressure and restart the timing from the beginning** — the full original process time. Safety-critical.
- Time up: heat off, remove from burner if possible, **let the canner depressurize naturally. DO NOT force-cool.** Cooling with cold running water or opening the vent before full depressurization causes liquid loss and seal failures — and **forced cooling may result in unsafe food** (the residual-heat cooldown is part of the calculated process).
- Wait until the vent-lock piston drops (newer canners), or ~30 min for pints / ~45 min for quarts (older heavy-walled canners). Remove the weight, **wait 10 more minutes**, then unfasten and lift the lid away from your face.
- Cool jars 12–24 hours undisturbed; test seals; store without bands (see §2.5).

**Dial vs weighted gauge:**
- **Weighted-gauge:** self-regulating (rocks/jiggles), needs no watching or accuracy testing. Above 1,000 ft: use **15 psi instead of 10** (or 10 instead of 5).
- **Dial-gauge:** allows fine control but the gauge **must be tested for accuracy yearly** (county Cooperative Extension offices do this; also after drops/submersion/rust). **A gauge that reads high causes under-processing → unsafe food.** If off by ≤ 2 psi, compensation is possible; if off by **more than 2 psi, replace the gauge.**
- The canner must hold **at least 4 quart jars** upright to count as a pressure canner for USDA processes.

### 2.4 Altitude tables

**The companion never gives process parameters without asking for the user's elevation.** If the user won't give it, the companion withholds the numbers rather than guessing.

**Water bath — add TIME** (water boils cooler at altitude):

| Altitude | Extra processing time |
|---|---|
| 1,001–3,000 ft | +5 minutes |
| 3,001–6,000 ft | +10 minutes |
| 6,001–8,000 ft | +15 minutes |
| 8,001–10,000 ft | +20 minutes |

**Pressure canning — raise PRESSURE, not time** (same numbers across all USDA recipe tables):

| Altitude | Dial-gauge | Weighted-gauge |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1,000 ft | 11 psi | 10 psi |
| 1,001–2,000 ft | 11 psi | 15 psi |
| 2,001–4,000 ft | 12 psi | 15 psi |
| 4,001–6,000 ft | 13 psi | 15 psi |
| 6,001–8,000 ft | 14 psi | 15 psi |

(Dial-gauge rule in short form: 11/12/13/14 psi per 2,000-ft band. Weighted-gauge rule: 10 psi at ≤1,000 ft, **15 psi above 1,000 ft**.)

### 2.5 Seals, storage, reprocessing

**Three seal tests** (after the 12–24-hour cool, bands removed):
1. Press the middle of the lid — if it **springs up**, unsealed.
2. Tap with a teaspoon — **clear high-pitched ring** = sealed; dull = unsealed.
3. Eye-level look — **concave (curved down)** = sealed; flat or bulging = suspect.

**Unsealed jars:** reprocess **within 24 hours** with a new lid and the full process time, refrigerate and use within **one week**, or freeze (1″ headspace, up to one year).
**Hard gate:** reprocessing is only valid if the first attempt followed a **tested recipe**. If it didn't — all jars, sealed or not, may be unsafe: **discard**.

**Storage (NCHFP):**
- **Remove the rings.** A ring left on can hold a lid in place after the seal fails, **hiding the failure** — this is explicit NCHFP doctrine and one of the most common dangerous home-canning habits. Wash bands, protect from moisture, reuse them next time.
- Label and date every jar. Store **cool, dark, dry — 50–70°F is best**. Never above 95°F, near hot pipes/range/furnace, in an uninsulated attic, or in direct sunlight (dampness corrodes lids and breaks seals). Don't stack more than two layers high, with a firm supportive layer between tiers.
- **Best quality within 1 year** — "can no more food than you will use within a year."

### 2.6 Recipe rules (travel with every canning session)

- **Tested recipes only:** USDA Complete Guide, NCHFP, current state-extension. Never family/blog/AI-generated recipes; never "approve a swap."
- Only **5% acidity (50-grain) commercial vinegar**; never reduce vinegar or dilute it unless the recipe says so; never homemade vinegar of unknown acidity.
- **No starch, flour, pasta, rice, cream, milk, or thickeners** added to canning recipes (NCHFP Soups page explicit ban — thicker product heats slower; botulism risk).
- **No larger jars than the recipe specifies.**
- Safe pickle-recipe changes the companion may cite (NDSU): reduce sugar or salt in quick-process pickles; swap 5%-for-5% vinegars; add a clove of garlic or small dried hot pepper; calcium chloride for crispness. Everything else is off-limits.

### 2.7 Unsafe methods — hard nos with the why

- **Oven "canning":** dry heat cracks jars (glass jars are not oven-safe — can shatter) and transfers heat poorly.
- **Open kettle:** no kill step for organisms entering the jar during filling; weak seals.
- **Dishwasher / microwave processing:** not tested, inconsistent heat.
- **Electric multicookers ("canning" button):** CDC says do not use them for canning. NCHFP recipes are developed for stovetop canners holding 4+ quart jars.
- **Steam canners:** unverified this pass — the kit stays with water bath + pressure unless resolved.
- **Mayo/commercial jars in a pressure canner:** not recommended (narrower sealing surface, weaker temper, scratch risk).

## 3. Freezing — companion-relevant detail

### Quality-vs-safety (FSIS)

**"Food stored constantly at 0°F will always be safe. Only the quality suffers with lengthy freezer storage."** (FSIS). Freezing keeps food safe by preventing microorganism growth — it does **not** kill bacteria; once thawed they can become active. Frozen times are never expiration dates.

**Quality ceilings (FSIS cold-storage chart + NCHFP):** ground meat 3–4 mo · steaks/chops/roasts 4–12 mo · whole chicken/turkey 1 yr / pieces 9 mo · lean fish 6 mo / fatty fish 2–3 mo · cooked fish 4–6 mo · bacon 1 mo · raw sausage 1–2 mo · cooked meat dishes/leftovers/casseroles 2–3 mo · soups/stews/gravy/broth 2–3 mo · fruits & vegetables 8–12 mo · raw egg yolks/whites 1 yr (in shell: do not freeze).

### Packaging (NCHFP)

Moisture-vapor-resistant, freezer-grade materials (bags, wraps, boxes made *for freezing*); **pack tightly, remove as much air as possible**; most foods need **headspace for expansion** in rigid containers (exceptions: loosely packed vegetables like asparagus/broccoli, bony meat pieces, tray-packed foods, breads); cool foods before packaging; **label name + date**; freeze in meal-size quantities; don't overload the freezer with unfrozen food.

### Freezer burn (NCHFP FAQ)

Dehydration of the surface from improper wrapping. **"The food is safe to eat but poorer quality."** Prevent: airtight, air-free packaging. Remedy: trim and repurpose — don't discard safe food.

### Thawing + refreezing (FSIS — four safe paths)

Fridge (safest; ~1 day per 5 lb for turkey) · cold water in a **leakproof bag**, water **changed every 30 minutes**, **cook immediately** · microwave, **cook immediately** · cook from frozen (~**1.5× time**).
**Never:** countertop, garage, basement, car, dishwasher, garbage bag, outdoors, porch.

**Refreeze:** fridge-thawed = safe to refreeze uncooked (quality loss only). Cold-water/microwave-thawed = **cook first**. Out >2 hrs (>1 hr above 90°F) = **throw out**, never refreeze. After a power outage: refreeze only if ice crystals remain **or** the freezer stayed at **≤40°F** (full freezer holds ~48 hrs, half-full ~24 hrs, door unopened).

### Foods that don't freeze well (NCHFP — quality list, not safety)

Limp/raw salad greens, mealy cooked potatoes, mushy cooked pasta/rice, rubbery cooked egg whites, separated cream/milk sauces and sour cream, separated mayonnaise, weeping gelatin. Never present these as unsafe — they're texture failures only.

## 4. Dehydrating — companion-relevant detail

### The drying race (USDA/FSIS "Jerky and Food Safety")

Maintaining **130–140°F** in the dehydrator during drying matters because: the process must be **fast enough to dry food before it spoils**, and must remove enough water that microorganisms can't grow. Too low → never dries (microbes grow in wet food). Too high → **case hardening**: the outside hardens and traps moisture inside, which spoils later. Fruits/vegetables: **~140°F** optimum. Never much above ~165°F during the dry.

### Jerky — the USDA temperature rule

Meat to **160°F internal / poultry to 165°F internal — BEFORE or AFTER dehydrating**, verified with a thermometer. Why dehydrating alone fails: most dehydrator heat goes into evaporating moisture, so the meat's temperature doesn't rise until most moisture is gone — by then the bacteria are **more heat resistant** and likelier to survive. Homemade jerky has caused *Salmonella* and *E. coli* O157:H7 illness.

Two approved methods (Ohio State Extension): **pre-cook** (USDA-recommended safest — simmer/steam in marinade or bake at 325°F on wire racks to 160°F/165°F internal), or **post-drying heat** (dried strips single-layer at 275°F oven until internal 160°F/165°F). Prep: slice ≤1/4″, trim all fat, keep meat ≤40°F, defrost in the fridge (never the counter), marinate for flavor then heat.

### Dryness, conditioning, storage (NCHFP)

- Fruits: <20% moisture — not tacky, still pliable, no moisture squeezes out when cut. Cool 30–60 min before packaging.
- Vegetables: brittle/"crisp," ~10% moisture — no conditioning needed.
- Jerky: hard but pliable, no moisture or stickiness after cooling.
- **Conditioning** (fruit only): pack cooled fruit loosely in jars, seal, stand **7–10 days, shake daily**; condensation = return to the dehydrator. Conditioning equalizes moisture and reduces mold risk.
- **Moldy dried food is discarded.** Food that absorbed moisture but isn't spoiled: use immediately or re-dry and repackage.
- Storage: cool completely first (warm food sweats → mold); clean, dry, insect-proof containers; cool, dark, dry. Fruits: **~1 yr at 60°F, ~6 mo at 80°F**; vegetables about half of fruits.
- *Verification note: the USDA jerky page wording came via an archived mirror — see §6.*

## 5. Fermentation — companion-relevant detail

### Salt is pathogen control (Penn State / NDSU)

The tested sauerkraut ratio — **3 Tbsp canning/pickling salt per 5 lb shredded cabbage** (~2.25%) — is explicitly what **"controls pathogen growth. Changing the proportions could result in an unsafe product."** (Penn State Extension). NDSU: you may **not** change the salt amount or type for fermented vegetables (sauerkraut, genuine dill/crock pickles). Reduced-sodium salts are not recommended for ferments. **The companion never invents a fermentation salt percentage** — it cites the tested recipe's number.

The FDA sauerkraut standard of identity (2–3% salt) was seen only via a secondary regulatory summary; the Penn State tested ratio is the companion's anchor.

### The submerged rule

Vegetables must stay **under the brine**: pack, pound, or squeeze until the drawn juices cover the surface; weigh down with a plate or water-filled sealed bags. The anaerobic environment is what lets lactic-acid bacteria win.

### Temperature (USDA Guide 6 via Penn State)

**70–75°F for 3–4 weeks**; 55–65°F is acceptable (takes 5–6 weeks); **avoid above 80°F** — pickles get too soft. Bubbling should begin within 24–48 hours.

### Normal vs. spoilage (NCHFP / USDA Guide 6)

- Normal: bubbling/gas in the first 48 hours; cloudy brine; surface scum (wild yeasts) that you **skim promptly**, checking several times a week.
- Spoilage → **discard**: soft, slimy, foul-odor pickles; spurting liquid; mold; mushiness; slipperiness; bulging lids; loss of seal. **"If the pickles become soft, slimy, or develop a disagreeable odor, discard them."**
- Fully fermented pickles: **4–6 months refrigerated** in the original container (skim scum/mold regularly). Canning fully fermented pickles (tested procedure) is the better long-term storage.

### Food-safety boundaries

- **Low-salt experiments:** cutting salt to taste = an untested recipe. Low-acid + anaerobic + low-salt is the exact condition set in which *C. botulinum* can grow and produce neurotoxin.
- **Garlic/herbs in oil at room temperature:** botulism risk — see §6/pickles below. Oil is anaerobic; garlic and herbs are low-acid and can carry *C. botulinum* spores. **Refrigerate (~4 days) or freeze.** Linked to ≥3 North American outbreaks. Never freelance an acidification procedure.
- **Homemade fermented/cured meats:** out of scope for beginner guidance — requires validated ≥5-log pathogen reduction with starter culture, dextrose, nitrite/nitrate (USDA/FSIS). Redirect to extension/university resources; never improvise.

## 6. Pickles — the fridge vs shelf-stable bright line

### Refrigerator (quick) pickles

**A short-term refrigerated food, NOT canned goods.** They are fresh-packed into jars, **not** water-bath processed or fermented, and **"cannot be stored safely at room temperature."** Keep refrigerated, use short-term — follow the tested recipe's stated time. (Exact numbers vary across extension sources — two weeks per Wyoming Extension; longer in some guidance — so the companion says "short-term refrigerated use per the recipe," not a single number.)

### Shelf-stable pickles

Two load-bearing elements, both from tested recipes:
1. **Correct acid:** vinegar at **5% acidity**; **"Never alter a recipe's proportions of vinegar, food, or water"** — there must be a minimum uniform acid level throughout to prevent botulinal growth. Below-5% vinegar "is not adequate to control microbial growth" (Penn State).
2. **Processing:** acid foods **canned in a boiling-water bath** — processing destroys yeasts/molds/bacteria and forms the vacuum seal.

**Why you can't just "can" a fridge-pickle recipe:** shelf-stable safety rests on the tested combination of acid ratio, ingredient ratios, and validated process time. A fridge-pickle recipe's brine ratio, acidity, and preparation were **never validated** for room-temperature storage. Putting a lid on it changes nothing — it's still a refrigerator pickle sitting at an unsafe temperature.

**Never re-use pickle brine** — after soaking vegetables it's "now of unknown acidity" (NCHFP via K-State).

### Garlic and herbs in oil — detail

Oregon State Extension: low-acid foods (garlic, mushrooms, chili peppers, fresh herbs) in oil create an oxygen-free environment "perfect for growth of *Clostridium botulinum*." Commercial garlic-in-oil is acidified to a tested standard; homemade is not. Rule: **refrigerate (use within ~4 days, strictest reading) or freeze for longer storage.** State the range honestly (some older guidance allowed longer) and **lead conservative: 4 days / freeze for longer.**

## 7. Storage, spoilage, and suspect-food handling

### Storage rules (NCHFP)

- **Rings OFF stored jars** — explicit NCHFP doctrine: a ring holds a flat lid in place after the seal fails, hiding the failure. Wash, dry, remove bands.
- **50–70°F, cool/dark/dry.** Nothing above 95°F, near hot pipes/range/furnace, in an uninsulated attic, or in direct sunlight. Dampness corrodes lids and breaks seals.
- Label and date every jar. Don't stack more than two layers high, firm layer between tiers (a manufacturer's suggestion — practical guidance, not a tested safety parameter).
- **Use within 1 year** for best quality ("can no more food than you will use within a year"). Dried: fruits ~1 yr at 60°F / ~6 mo at 80°F; vegetables about half. Refrigerated ferments: ~4–6 months.
- Freezer: **0°F or below**; food is safe indefinitely at 0°F; published times are quality-only.

### The pre-use inspection (NCHFP "Identifying and Handling Spoiled Canned Food")

Before opening each jar: check the lid for **tightness and vacuum** (concave center). Rotate the upright jar and look for **streaks of dried food from the top**, **rising air bubbles**, **unnatural color**. On opening: **unnatural odors**, **spurting liquid**, **cotton-like mold** (white, blue, black, green) on the food surface or lid underside. Bulging/swollen lids, leaking/stained, broken seal, cracked jar, discolored food = suspect.

### The critical asymmetry

**Botulinum toxin is odorless, colorless, and tasteless.** Low-acid foods may contain botulinum toxin **without showing signs of spoilage**. Therefore **all suspect containers of low-acid foods (including tomatoes) are treated as if they have produced botulinum toxin** (NCHFP).

### Suspect-food handling — sealed vs. unsealed (NCHFP; keep the distinction)

- **Never taste-test a questionable jar.** "Do not taste food from a jar with an unsealed lid or food that shows signs of spoilage." Discard swollen, bulging, leaking, or cracked containers **without tasting**. Do **not** feed suspect food to pets; don't bury it where animals reach it; keep it from children.
- **Still-sealed suspect containers** (swollen/suspect cans or jars): place in a heavy garbage bag, close it, into the regular trash or landfill. **No boiling.**
- **Unsealed, open, or leaking suspect containers** → detoxify before disposal:
  1. Disposable rubber or heavy-plastic gloves on.
  2. Containers + lids on their sides in an 8-quart (or larger) pot; cover with water (≥1 inch above).
  3. Lid on the pot; **boil 30 minutes** to detoxify food and container components.
  4. Cool; discard containers, lids, and food in the trash/landfill.
- **Cleanup:** fresh solution of **1 part unscented household chlorine bleach (5–6%) to 5 parts clean water**; wet contaminated surfaces, let stand **30 minutes**; wipe with paper towels (bag and trash them); re-apply for another 30 minutes; rinse. Bleach is an irritant — don't inhale it or get it on skin.

### The one legitimate pre-eat boil (NCHFP/UGA Extension)

Home-canned **low-acid** foods from a **trusted procedure** where there is ***doubt about the process*** — not signs of spoilage — can be made safer by **boiling 10 minutes before eating** (+1 minute per 1,000 ft elevation). **It never makes visibly spoiled food edible.** Boiling destroys the toxin but **does not make spoiled food safe to eat** — detoxification boiling is for **disposal only**.

## 8. Verification gaps (what still needs a live-source re-check)

The companion encodes these as labeled gaps, never as facts:

1. **Jerky page wording vs live FSIS.** The USDA/FSIS "Jerky and Food Safety" text used in research came via an archived mirror (cybercemetery.unt.edu). Cross-check against the current FSIS jerky page before the companion teaches jerky prep verbatim.
2. **Blanching per-vegetable pages.** The NCHFP blanching times table is cited rather than reprinted; the ice-water cooling wording was not captured verbatim; and the "no blanching needed for tomatoes/peppers/onions" chart contradicts NCHFP's own per-vegetable times. One pass over NCHFP's per-vegetable freezing pages is recommended.
3. **FoodKeeper numbers vs a USDA-hosted source.** Fridge/freezer storage-time numbers came via USDA-header-verbatim mirrors (FoodKeeper app page itself was unreachable). Confirm against the live FoodKeeper app or a USDA-hosted PDF — including the currently omitted condiment rows (opened ketchup/mustard/dressings) and yogurt, which have **no primary-source numbers yet** and ship with "check the label" until then.
4. **Tomato acidification spoon measures.** The standard measures (bottled lemon juice / citric acid per pint and quart) are widely repeated but were **not confirmed from a primary source** this pass. Verify against NCHFP's tomato pages before the companion quotes them. Until then: the companion sends users to the tested recipe's own measures.
5. **Atmospheric steam canners.** USDA's NAL exhibit lists boiling-water and pressure as the only recommended methods; NCHFP has separate steam-canner guidance that was not verified. Unresolved — kit stays with the two classic methods.
6. **Thaw/refreeze detail paragraphs.** The detailed thaw and refreeze text came via archived FSIS PDFs ("Freezing and Food Safety," "The Big Thaw") with the core claims corroborated on the live FSIS "Keep Food Safe!" page — re-verify the details against a live fsis.usda.gov URL in the next pass.
7. **General headspace table by food type** (¼″ jams/jellies, ½″ fruits/tomatoes, 1–1¼″ low-acid/meats) — seen in NCHFP per-recipe instructions but not confirmed as a standalone general rule. Verify against USDA Guide 1 before the kit teaches defaults.
8. **Smoker-specific fire guidance.** No authoritative fire-department page specific to smokers was found; the skill's smoker-fire guidance extends fire-service grill rules by analogy. Check manufacturer safety manuals (Traeger, Weber, Pit Boss) and any fire-department smoker bulletins in a future pass.
9. **Burn-severity triage and first-aid wording.** Burn-cooling numbers come from the American Red Cross Scientific Advisory Council's 2019 advisory; detailed severity triage (palm rule, location-based rules) and the cut-care wording were not re-verified against redcross.org directly — the skill keeps the wording **without** citing redcross.org. Re-verify before sharpening triage language.
10. **The "4-hour rule."** There is none in USDA consumer guidance — it's an FDA Food Code commercial concept (cooling 135→70°F in 2 hours, then to 41°F within 6 total). The kit teaches only the 2-hour (1-hour above 90°F) rule and names the 4-hour concept as retail-only.
