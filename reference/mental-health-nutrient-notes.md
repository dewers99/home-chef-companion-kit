# Food and Mental Health — Nutrient Notes — Home Chef Companion Kit v1.0.0

**Reference appendix.** The nutrient-by-nutrient patchwork behind anything the companion says about food and mood. Consulted only when the user asks for the evidence behind a claim. Grades: **strong RCT** = replicated RCTs in clinical populations · **observational** = associations, cannot show causation · **preliminary** = small/single/short trials · **contested** = legitimate expert disagreement or conflicting meta-analyses.

**Standing note:** the evidence supports food as a *possible adjunct* to professional mental-health care — never as a substitute. Nothing in this file is a clinical recommendation, and the companion never suggests diet replaces treatment or medication.

---

## 1. Dietary patterns and mood

**The consistent signal:** Mediterranean-style / whole-food dietary patterns are *associated* with lower depression risk and show *small RCT-tested benefits* for depressive symptoms — always as an adjunct to standard care.

- **SMILES trial (2017) — strong RCT (single trial, with limitations):** 12-week parallel-group RCT, n=67 adults with moderate-to-severe major depression and poor baseline diets. Intervention: 7 individual sessions with a clinical dietitian prescribing a modified Mediterranean diet ("ModiMed"); control: social-support ("befriending") protocol. 55 of 67 were already in therapy or on medication — **diet was tested as an adjunct, not a replacement.** Findings: significantly greater MADRS improvement (p < 0.001), Cohen's d = −1.16 (large), remission (MADRS < 10) 32.3% vs. 8.0%, NNT = 4.1. Limitations: only 67 enrolled (well short of the planned sample — the authors note the small sample "may have inflated the effect size"), participants couldn't be blinded, differential dropout (94% vs. 73.5% completion). A critic (Molendijk) argues the trials don't exceed placebo expectations in antidepressant trials. **Honest read: one small but well-conducted RCT with a large-but-likely-inflated effect; replication needed.**
- **HELFIMED (2019) — strong RCT (single trial, limitations):** 6-month parallel RCT, n=152 enrolled (85 completed 6-month assessments), adults with **self-reported** (not clinically diagnosed) depression. Intervention: Mediterranean-diet cooking workshops + food hampers + fish-oil supplements (6 months); control: social groups. Greater depression reduction at 3 months (p = 0.03), sustained at 6 months. Limitations: **diet and fish oil were confounded** (the fish oil was ~900 mg DHA / 200 mg EPA — the reverse of the ratio most associated with mood effects); high attrition (~44% by 6 months); participants got free food (a mood-relevant confounder in itself).
- **AMMEND (2022) — preliminary:** 12-week RCT, n=72 young men (18–25) with moderate-to-severe depression and poor diets; Mediterranean dietary support vs. befriending. BDI-II improvement favored diet by 14.4 points; 36% of the diet group reached full remission. Small, single demographic, open-label.
- **Firth et al. (2019) — strong RCT (meta-analysis):** 16 dietary-intervention RCTs, n=45,826. Dietary interventions reduced depressive symptoms with a **small** pooled effect: Hedges' g = 0.275 (p = 0.002) — held in high-quality trials and vs. both inactive and active controls. **No significant effect on anxiety** (g = 0.100, p = 0.148). All diet types performed similarly — **no evidence that a specific named diet outperforms general dietary improvement.** Crucial context: only 1 of 16 trials enrolled a clinically depressed sample. **The pooled RCT effect is small — SMILES' d = −1.16 is the outlier, not the norm.**
- **Observational cohorts:** Lassale et al. (2019) meta-analysis — high vs. low Mediterranean adherence: RR = 0.67 for depression incidence across 4 longitudinal studies. REGICOR 2024 (n=3,046): 16% lower odds of depressive disorder per 1-SD increase in Mediterranean score. Umbrella reviews grade this observational evidence as low-to-moderate certainty — consistent direction, heavily confounded (exercise, social connection, socioeconomic status, reverse causation).

**What the companion may say when asked:** "Studies link a Mediterranean-style pattern to better mood outcomes, and trials show small symptom improvements as part of overall care." Never: "this recipe reduces depression by X%."

## 2. Gut-brain axis / microbiome — observational + preliminary

**What the field knows:** the gut-brain axis is a real bidirectional signaling network (vagus nerve, HPA axis, immune signaling, microbial metabolites like short-chain fatty acids). Germ-free mouse studies show microbiome changes alter behavior; fecal transplant from depressed humans into mice can transfer depression-like behaviors — fascinating, but mice are not people, and these studies don't establish human treatment effects.

**Human evidence:** microbiota–depression associations are documented (Gao et al. 2023 systematic review/meta-analysis) but confounded by diet, medication, and lifestyle. Probiotics ("psychobiotics") are preliminary: a 2021 review of 16 RCTs (n=1,125) found some depression measures improved, others didn't; a 2025 review of 23 RCTs in clinically diagnosed depression/anxiety found depressive-symptom reductions with substantial between-study variability; individual strains (e.g., *L. helveticus* R0052 + *B. longum* R0175) show consistent signals across a few small trials. Caveats: high heterogeneity, strain-specific effects, small samples, short durations, publication-bias risk. **No probiotic is approved or established as a depression treatment; guidelines do not recommend them for mood disorders.**

**What the companion may say:** "Fiber and fermented foods support a healthy gut microbiome, an active area of mental-health research." Never: "probiotic X will fix your depression" or "heal your gut to cure anxiety." The dietary-pattern evidence is currently more actionable than any specific probiotic product.

## 3. Nutrients, graded

### Omega-3s (EPA/DHA) — strong RCT (adjunctive), with caveats

- Liao et al. (2019, *Translational Psychiatry*) meta-analysis of RCTs: omega-3s with **EPA ≥ 60%** at ≤1 g/day EPA showed beneficial effects on depression (EPA-pure subgroup SMD ≈ −0.48 to −1.03 depending on model). **DHA-dominant formulations showed no significant effect.** Earlier meta-analyses converge: EPA-dominant formulas help depressive symptoms; effect sizes vary (SMD ~0.3–1.0). Caveats: high heterogeneity (I² often ~75%); mostly adjunctive use alongside standard treatment; evidence strongest for EPA, not DHA.
- **Companion-safe framing:** oily fish, walnuts, flax are part of the Mediterranean pattern. Food-based omega-3s (oily fish) are uncontroversial. No supplement claims, no doses. (Note the contradiction: HELFIMED supplemented with a DHA-dominant fish oil — the opposite of the evidence-based ratio — and couldn't separate diet from supplement anyway.)

### B vitamins (folate, B6, B12) — observational + preliminary (mixed)

- Observational: low serum folate associated with ~35% higher odds of depression (pooled OR = 1.35, meta-analysis of meta-analyses). Low B12/folate linked to depression in older adults. Reverse causation is real — depression can *cause* poor intake.
- RCTs disagree: B12 supplementation meta-analysis (16 RCTs, n=6,276; Markun et al. 2021) found **no overall effect on depressive symptoms** in people without overt deficiency. A large RCT of folic acid + B6 + B12 in 4,331 women over ~7 years (Okereke et al., JAMA) found **no significant effect on depression risk**, including high-risk subgroups. Folate as *add-on* to antidepressants shows some small signals in small trials — not established.
- **Companion-safe framing:** correct deficiency with a clinician's guidance; don't sell B-complex as a mood intervention. Preliminary at best.

### Vitamin D — contested

- Some meta-analyses of RCTs find small-to-moderate depressive-symptom reductions (SMD ≈ −0.3 to −0.4; one dose-response analysis); others find **no significant effect** (older adults: SMD −0.49, p = 0.10 pre-post; post-score SMD −0.10, p = 0.25 — null). GRADE certainty is typically very low to moderate; heterogeneity extreme (I² 79–88%); effects are short-term and not seen for anxiety.
- **Companion-safe framing:** contested. Do not present vitamin D as a depression treatment. Testing and supplementation are a doctor's call.

### Magnesium — preliminary

- Observational association between low magnesium intake/status and depression; causality unclear.
- One notable RCT: Tarleton et al. (2017, *PLoS ONE*) — open-label, randomized, cross-over, n=126 (112 analyzable), mild-to-moderate depression; 248 mg elemental magnesium/day (magnesium chloride) × 6 weeks improved PHQ-9 by −6.0 points (p < 0.001) and GAD-7 by −4.5 points, effects within 2 weeks, well tolerated. Limitations: **open-label, no placebo**, single trial, not replicated at scale. Promising but preliminary.
- **Companion-safe framing:** magnesium-rich foods (leafy greens, nuts, legumes, whole grains) are part of a Mediterranean pattern. No supplement claims.

## 4. Contradictions flagged (not resolved)

1. **SMILES effect size vs. pooled RCT effect size.** SMILES: d = −1.16; Firth et al.'s 16-trial pool: g = 0.275. Jacka's team acknowledges the small sample may inflate the effect. Molendijk argues the result doesn't beat placebo expectations. Present SMILES as one promising small trial within a field whose overall pooled effect is small — never present the 32% remission figure as "what diet does."
2. **Vitamin D literature is internally contradictory.** Some meta-analyses find SMD ≈ −0.3 to −1.0; a well-conducted older-adult analysis finds null; GRADE certainty very low-to-moderate. Contested — no claims.
3. **B-vitamin observational vs. RCT evidence conflict.** Low folate/B12 *associated* with depression (OR 1.35); supplementation RCTs in non-deficient populations show no benefit. Correct deficiency via a clinician; don't market B vitamins for mood.
4. **Omega-3: EPA works, DHA doesn't (in the data).** DHA-dominant supplements show no significant effect. HELFIMED used a DHA-dominant oil anyway — and couldn't separate diet from supplement.
5. **Anxiety evidence is thin.** Firth et al. found no significant dietary effect on anxiety; probiotic umbrella reviews find anxiety results "considerably less consistent" than depression. The companion is most cautious about anxiety claims of all.
6. **The "90% of serotonin is made in the gut" talking point.** True in the narrow sense (enterochromaffin cells produce most of the body's serotonin) but routinely misused to imply dietary changes directly raise brain serotonin. Peripheral serotonin doesn't cross the blood–brain barrier. The companion does not use this as a mechanism claim.
