# MS and Diet — Research Notes — Home Chef Companion Kit v1.0.0

**Reference appendix.** The evidence behind the MS behavior rules in the core skill. Consulted only when the user asks what the research says. Evidence grades: **small RCT** = randomized but small and/or short; **observational** = association only; **preliminary** = pilots/single-arm; **contested** = evidence points both ways or claims exceed data. Behavior rules live in the core skill — this file is evidence only.

**Standing position (National MS Society, verified from nationalmssociety.org):** "No 'Best Diet' for People Living With MS… Scientists have **not found a definitive diet that can change the course of MS**." NMSS recommends people with MS follow healthy-eating guidance for the general population (e.g., the U.S. Dietary Guidelines) unless their care team advises otherwise.

## 1. The Swank diet (low saturated fat)

Developed in the 1950s by neurologist Roy Swank. Limits saturated fat to ≤20 g/day (later ≤15 g/day), total fat ≤40 g/day; emphasizes fruits, vegetables, whole grains, lean protein. Based on Swank's observation that MS was rarer in low-saturated-fat regions (e.g., Norway).

**The original study — observational, with strong caveats:**
- Swank followed 144 MS patients for 34 years (*The Lancet*, 1990; extended follow-up to 50 years reported 2003). Patients who adhered to the ≤20 g saturated-fat limit had less disability progression and lower death rates (about 31% vs. >80% among "poor dieters"). In the subgroup with minimal disability at study entry, 95% of adherent patients were reported to survive and remain physically active.
- **Limitations:** no control group, no randomization, no blinding of assessors. "Good" vs. "poor" dieter comparison risks reverse causality — people who feel well find it easier to stick to the diet; people whose disease worsens may abandon it — so the comparison may just track who was already doing better. No brain MRI data (MRI wasn't available for most of the study period), no standardized dietary assessment, risk of non-random missing data.
- **Grade: observational / contested as a treatment claim.** Intriguing long-term association; the design cannot establish cause. Some patient-facing summaries phrase the findings ("95% did not experience progression") without these caveats — use the peer-reviewed critique (Snetselaar et al., *Nutrients* 2019 review) as the corrective.

**Later, better-designed Swank work:**
- Yadav et al., 2016 — randomized, n=61, 12 months: a very-low-fat plant-based (McDougall-style) version vs. control — **small RCT**: reduced fatigue, weight loss, improved cholesterol/insulin. No significant MRI or quality-of-life differences vs. control.
- The WAVES trial (below) tested a Swank arm in a randomized parallel design.

## 2. The Wahls protocol (modified Paleolithic)

Created by physician Terry Wahls (who has secondary-progressive MS). The WahlsElim diet: 6–9 cups/day of vegetables and fruits (greens, sulfur-rich, intensely colored), meat and fish; excludes grains, dairy, eggs, legumes, nightshades, and gluten-free grains. The full protocol pairs the diet with supplements, exercise/neuromuscular electrical stimulation, meditation, and massage. A less-restrictive "Wahls Paleo" level also exists.

**The studies:**
1. **Bisht et al., 2014 — pilot (n=10 completing of 13 enrolled, 12 months):** single-arm, open-label, **no control group**. Fatigue Severity Scale dropped from 5.7 to 3.32 on average. Multimodal bundle (diet + supplements + exercise/e-stim + meditation + massage) — the effect cannot be attributed to the diet alone. **Grade: preliminary.** (Disclose the conflict: the lead author created the diet.)
2. **Bisht et al., 2015 — pilot extension (n=20 progressive MS, 12 months):** reduced fatigue, improved quality of life; greater adherence linked to greater improvement. Still no control group, no randomization, small. **Grade: preliminary.**
3. **WAVES trial, 2021 (Wahls et al.) — randomized, n=87 RRMS, 24 weeks:** randomized **parallel-arm** comparing the Swank diet vs. the Wahls elimination diet — note **both arms are active diets; there is no no-diet control group**. Findings: **both** diets were associated with clinically meaningful *within-group* reductions in fatigue (FSS, MFIS) and improved quality-of-life scores at 12 and 24 weeks. Mental QoL improved in the Wahls arm but not the Swank arm; neither group improved 6-minute walk distance. **Conflicts of interest:** the lead author created the Wahls diet. Small sample, short duration, no usual-care control. **Grade: small RCT** — supports "both patterns may reduce perceived fatigue" more than "Wahls beats alternatives."
4. **Irish et al., 2017 — randomized, n=34 RRMS, 3 months:** modified Paleolithic vs. control — reduced fatigue and improved MS-related QoL; but very small, short, and only 8/17 intervention and 9/17 control participants' data were analyzable due to adherence issues. **Grade: small RCT / preliminary.**

**Limitations across the Wahls literature:** pilot work is uncontrolled and bundled (no single component's effect is isolable); RCTs are small and short; the highest-profile trial was led by the diet's creator. **No trial shows effects on relapses, disability progression, or MRI lesion activity.**

**Bottom line:** preliminary-to-small-RCT evidence that a Wahls-style pattern may reduce *perceived fatigue* and improve *self-reported quality of life*. Nothing establishes disease-modifying effects.

## 3. Vitamin D and MS — contested

**Observational links — grade: observational (consistent):** lower vitamin D levels and lower sunlight exposure are consistently associated with higher MS risk and, in people with MS, with higher relapse rates and more MRI activity. Mendelian-randomization studies (genetic proxies for vitamin D status) support a **causal link to MS susceptibility** (developing MS in the first place). Example: one uncontrolled observational study found each 10 nmol/L increase in 25-OH-D associated with ~13.7% lower relapse incidence.

**Interventional evidence — grade: contested:**
- 2013 meta-analysis of 5 small RCTs (n=254): no significant effect of high-dose vitamin D on relapse risk (OR 0.98, 95% CI 0.45–2.16).
- 2021 meta-analysis: "vitamin D supplements (high or low dose) have no significant effect on relapse rate and disability during treatment."
- September 2026 systematic review (*Brain and Behavior*, 11 studies incl. 8 RCTs): inconsistent evidence — "High-dose supplementation for disease modification is not supported by current data."
- June 2026 meta-analysis of 32 RCTs (n=2,254, mostly add-on to disease-modifying therapies): long-term (>1 yr) supplementation associated with ~20% lower relapse risk and modest disability-score improvement — newer, conflicts with earlier meta-analyses, needs independent replication.
- Practical consensus in reviews: most people with MS are vitamin D deficient, so avoiding deficiency per general guidelines (levels monitored by the care team) is prudent general health advice — **not an MS treatment**.

**Bottom line:** worth not being deficient in, like everyone else; the evidence that supplementation changes MS course is genuinely contested.

## 4. Other named approaches studied in MS

| Approach | Key evidence | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Mediterranean diet | Sand et al., 2019 (n=36, 6 months, randomized): less fatigue, weight loss, improved QoL vs. control. Small, short. A 2026 systematic review: mixed evidence; some support for cognition. | Small RCT / contested |
| McDougall (very-low-fat vegan) | Yadav et al., 2016 (n=61, 12 months, randomized): reduced fatigue, weight loss, better cholesterol/insulin. No MRI/QoL differences. | Small RCT |
| Ketogenic diet | Brenton et al., 2019 (n=20, 6 months, no control): reduced fatigue, depression, disability. Choi et al., 2016 (n=60, 6 months, randomized with fasting-mimicking arm): good safety, improved QoL. NMSS notes long-term safety not established. | Preliminary / small RCT |
| Intermittent fasting / caloric restriction | Fitzgerald et al., 2018 (n=36, 8 weeks, randomized): weight loss, improved emotional well-being; no MS-specific effects. | Small RCT / preliminary |
| Overcoming MS (OMS) program | Plant-based + fish + flaxseed/fish oil + meditation/exercise. Observational survey data link the lifestyle program to better self-reported mental/physical QoL. | Observational |
| Gluten-free diet | One non-randomized self-selected study (Rodrigo et al., 2014, 4.5 yrs): less MRI activity, lower disability — high bias risk. NMSS: "unclear whether gluten has any relationship to MS." | Preliminary / contested |
| Dairy | Contradictory observational data: one study (n=2,087) found dairy avoidance linked to less disability; another (n=6,989) found higher dairy intake linked to *less* disability. NMSS: unclear if dairy is helpful, harmful, or neutral. | Contested |
| Sodium | One observational study (Farez et al., 2014) linked higher sodium to more relapses/lesions; two larger follow-ups found no association. | Contested |

**Common pattern:** small studies, short durations, fatigue/QoL/wellness outcomes — almost no trials powered to show effects on relapses, disability progression, or MRI outcomes.

## 5. Contradictions flagged (not resolved)

- **Vitamin D:** 2021 meta-analysis (no effect on relapse/disability) vs. June 2026 meta-analysis of 32 RCTs (~20% lower relapse risk) vs. September 2026 systematic review (inconsistent; high-dose not supported). Genuinely unsettled — present as contested. The 2026 papers were reached via news summaries, not full text; verify before citing specific numbers.
- **Dairy:** two observational studies point in opposite directions (n=2,087 vs. n=6,989). NMSS: unclear.
- **Sodium:** one study links higher sodium to more relapses/lesions; two larger follow-ups find no association. NMSS: unclear.
- **Cognition review (2026):** reports processing-speed improvements from Wahls/Swank studies but the authors explicitly caution that inconsistent designs and limited high-quality evidence "undermine strong recommendations."
