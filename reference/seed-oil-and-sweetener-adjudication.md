# Seed-Oil and Sweetener Adjudication — Home Chef Companion Kit v1.0.0

**Reference appendix.** Study-by-study breakdowns behind the neutral postures the companion takes on seed oils and contested ingredients. Consulted only when the user asks for the evidence behind a claim. The companion presents both sides graded — it never declares ingredients "toxic" or "perfectly safe" as settled fact.

## 1. Seed oils: the two narratives, study by study

**The popular narrative ("seed oils are toxic"):** soybean, canola/rapeseed, corn, sunflower, safflower, and grapeseed oils are high in omega-6 linoleic acid (LA). Claims: (a) LA converts to arachidonic acid → pro-inflammatory eicosanoids → chronic disease; (b) PUFAs oxidize at cooking temperatures into harmful compounds; (c) industrial extraction (hexane solvent, deodorization) leaves toxic residues.

**What the human evidence shows:**

| Claim | Evidence | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| LA raises systemic inflammation | Systematic review of RCTs (Su et al., 2017): deliberately *increasing* LA intake did not raise circulating inflammatory markers. Petersen et al. review: LA intake has negligible impact on blood/tissue arachidonic acid — the mechanistic premise of the inflammation claim doesn't hold in humans. **Funding caveat:** the Petersen review was funded by the Soy Nutrition Institute Global / United Soybean Board — cite it alongside independent data, never alone. | Strong (RCT) — claim not supported |
| Seed oils cause heart disease | 30-cohort pooled biomarker analysis (~69,000 people, 15,000+ CV events; Marklund et al., 2019, *Circulation*): higher blood LA → lower CVD, lower CV mortality, less ischemic stroke. 13-cohort meta-analysis (~310,000 people; Farvid et al., 2014, *Circulation*): highest-LA eaters had ~15% fewer coronary events, ~21% fewer coronary deaths. | Strong (observational) — opposite of the claim, replicated with objective biomarkers |
| Seed oils raise mortality | 44-cohort meta-analysis, 811,069 people (Li et al., 2020): higher LA intake → ~13% lower total mortality (RR ~0.87), lower CV and cancer mortality; held whether measured by diet surveys or blood/tissue biomarkers. | Strong (observational) — association, not proof of cause |
| Replacing saturated fat with PUFA oils helps | 2020 Cochrane meta-analysis, 15 RCTs, ~59,000 participants: replacing saturated fat with PUFA reduced combined cardiovascular events by ~21%. | Strong (RCT) |
| Hexane solvent residues are dangerous | Analyses of marketplace oils: a typical consumer would need to eat >100× average oil intake to reach even the lowest safety threshold (per Petersen et al. review). | Established (analytical data) — concern not supported at real exposures |
| Omega-6:omega-3 ratio framing | Researchers have largely moved away from the ratio; absolute intakes matter more, and the useful lever is raising omega-3 intake, not cutting omega-6. | Consensus shift — present as such |
| Oxidation at high heat | Chemically real — PUFA-rich oils oxidize more readily; smoke point is a practical (if imperfect) proxy. But **no demonstrated evidence that oxidation at home-cooking temperatures causes measurable harm in humans**. Reusing frying oil repeatedly is the scenario worth cautioning about. | Preliminary/contested — real chemistry, unproven health harm at home-cooking levels |

**Honest bottom line:** the "seed oils are toxic" narrative is **contested and contradicted** by the largest human datasets on cardiovascular outcomes; the mechanistic inflammation story doesn't replicate in RCTs. The one part worth acting on is the oxidation/heating behavior — a practical cooking concern, not a toxin claim. Also the misattribution problem: ultra-processed food *is* a real health problem (Hall et al., 2019 NIH RCT: an ultra-processed diet → +500 kcal/day and weight gain, macros matched) — but seed oils are one ingredient *in* ultra-processed food, not the proven cause of its harms.

**Practical rules the companion can give (culinary, not medical):** match oil to heat level — smoke point is a practical guide, not a health verdict; don't let oil smoke visibly and keep cooking in it; don't reuse deep-frying oil repeatedly; store oils away from heat and light (rancidity is real — off smells mean toss it). Values vary by brand, batch, and refinement — treat smoke points as ranges, not absolutes. High-oleic versions of safflower/sunflower have different fatty-acid profiles and higher stability — treat "sunflower oil" generically unless the label says high-oleic.

## 2. Soy fears: mostly debunked by RCT data

Why people avoid soy — the companion's first question should be *why*, because the handling differs (allergy = strict mode; phytoestrogen concerns = present the evidence, support the preference either way):

- **Soy allergy — established.** Soy is one of the nine major allergens (FASTER Act). Nuance: highly refined soybean oil is generally exempt from allergen labeling rules, but unrefined soy oil is not; fermented products (soy sauce, tamari, miso, tofu) still contain soy protein. Strict-mode swaps.

| Popular claim | What the evidence actually shows | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| "Soy lowers testosterone / feminizes men" | Meta-analysis of **41 RCTs** (Hamilton-Reeves et al., 2021, >1,750 men): no effect of soy protein or isoflavones on total T, free T, estradiol, estrone, or SHBG — regardless of dose or duration. An earlier 2010 meta-analysis reached the same conclusion. The fear traces to a handful of single-person case reports at extreme intakes. | Strong (RCT) — claim not supported |
| "Soy raises breast cancer risk" | Observational meta-analyses associate soy intake with *reduced* breast cancer risk (Asian cohorts with 25–50 mg/day isoflavones vs. ~1–2 mg/day in North America). Caveat: for people with estrogen-receptor-positive breast cancer or on drugs like tamoxifen, observational data can't guide individual decisions — oncologist territory, not kitchen territory. | Observational — risk-raising claim not supported; protective association, correlation only |
| "Soy damages thyroid" | 2019 meta-analysis of 18 RCTs (Otun et al., *Scientific Reports*): no significant change in free T3 or free T4; a *very modest* rise in TSH (WMD 0.248 mIU/L, p = 0.049, high heterogeneity, clinical significance "unclear" per the authors). People with diagnosed hypothyroidism on levothyroxine should keep soy away from their medication timing and ask their doctor — an absorption-timing issue, not a toxicity claim. | Strong (RCT) for normal thyroid function (no effect); the TSH finding is borderline and of unclear significance |
| "Soy is all GMO / heavily processed" | Mostly true of U.S. commodity soybeans, but that's a preference/provenance concern, not a toxicology claim. Organic/verified soy products exist. | N/A — preference, not evidence |

**The companion's line:** "The best available trials don't support the hormone fears — here's what the evidence grades as. Want swaps either way?" Never dismiss the user's preference — swaps work fine regardless of reason.

### Soy sauce swaps — exact status (verified against ingredient statements)

| Product | Sodium/tbsp (approx.) | Soy-free? | Gluten? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regular soy sauce | ~870–900 mg | No | Yes (wheat) |
| Low-sodium soy sauce | ~550 mg | No | Yes |
| Tamari | ~900–1,000 mg | **No** | Usually GF (certified on label) |
| Bragg Liquid Aminos (regular) | ~960 mg | **No** (soybeans + water) | Usually GF |
| Coconut aminos | ~198–270 mg | **Yes** (coconut sap) | None |

**Critical confusion points:** "gluten-free tamari" is still soy — soy-allergic users need coconut aminos. Bragg sells **two different aminos**: soy-based Liquid Aminos vs. soy-free Coconut Liquid Aminos — always disambiguate by full name. Coconut aminos is the only soy-free option of the swaps; it's sweeter and milder, so taste-adjust and add a pinch of salt or reduce other sweeteners. Swap ratios (culinary consensus): tamari or liquid aminos 1:1 for soy sauce (start slightly under); coconut aminos 1:1 by volume with taste-adjusting. Sodium figures vary by brand — always "check your label."

## 3. Nutritional yeast and psyllium — caution notes, graded

**Nutritional yeast ("nooch"):** deactivated *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* — cannot leaven anything. Cheesy/umami flavor from naturally occurring glutamates. ~2 tbsp ≈ 40–68 kcal (brand-dependent), ~10 g complete protein, ~4 g fiber, ~50 mg sodium. Health-benefit claims beyond that (blood sugar, immunity) rest on small/older trials only.

- **B12 — fortification-dependent only (established):** yeast doesn't produce B12; it appears on labels as an added fortificant (e.g., "cyanocobalamin"). Unfortified nooch has essentially no B12. The companion must ask or state which type is in play; for actual deficiency, B12 supplement/doctor guidance beats variable food sources.
- **Niacin load (established — UL):** fortified brands pack substantial niacin — one popular brand lists 35 mg niacin (220% DV) per 2 tbsp, another 25 mg (160% DV). The adult tolerable upper limit for niacin is 35 mg/day. Heavy daily users stacking multiple servings could approach or exceed it — worth a gentle note for supplement-stackers, not alarm. (Labels read via retailer listings — spot-check a physical package before publishing exact figures.)
- **Other cautions:** tyramine + MAOI antidepressants (established pharmacology — can spike blood pressure dangerously; check with a doctor); migraine sensitivity (preliminary); natural glutamate/MSG sensitivity (established composition, individual sensitivity); IBD/Crohn's — some limited, non-causal data suggest dietary yeast may worsen symptoms in a subset (preliminary); gout — purines → uric acid, moderate intake (established mechanism, individual risk varies); rare yeast allergy (established).
- **Unverified vendor claim — do not repeat:** one vendor blog claimed nooch is "not a good idea with hypertension or glaucoma" — no mechanism for glaucoma could be verified.

**Psyllium husk:** soluble gel-forming fiber from *Plantago ovata*; the standard gluten-stand-in in keto/low-carb/GF baking (~1–3 tbsp per recipe; mix into dry ingredients before liquids; powder ≈ 85% of whole husk; too little water → crumbly, too much → gummy). Well-established laxative and LDL-cholesterol lowering (strong RCT evidence). Caution: take with plenty of water (choking/blockage risk if taken dry — label warnings); psyllium sensitivity allergy = avoid entirely; medication timing — the drug-specific evidence is weak and partly animal/case-report based, so the companion's **only** move is the pharmacist referral, per the FDA label's own language ("Ask a doctor or pharmacist before use if you are taking any other drug. Take this product 2 or more hours before or after other drugs.").

## 4. Almond flour — tradeoffs (culinary consensus)

Finely ground blanched almonds (almond *meal* = unblanched, coarser — not interchangeable in delicate recipes). Naturally gluten-free, grain-free, low-carb; the workhorse keto/low-carb flour.

| Per serving | Almond flour (1 cup, ~96–112 g) | Coconut flour (¼ cup, ~30 g) | Wheat AP (1 cup, ~120 g) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calories | ~600–648 | ~120–131 | ~455 |
| Fat | 44–56 g (mostly unsaturated) | 4–5 g | ~1 g |
| Protein | ~24 g | 4–5 g | ~13 g |
| Net carbs | ~10–12 g | ~6–8 g | ~92 g |

**Tradeoffs the companion can state:** almond ↔ coconut flour is not 1:1 (~¼ cup coconut flour ≈ 1 cup almond flour, with significantly added liquid and extra eggs going toward coconut; a 3:1 almond-to-coconut blend is a commonly cited compromise). Calorie density is almond flour's real tradeoff — at ~600+ kcal/cup it's easy to overshoot calories on keto even with low net carbs. Rancidity: at ~50% fat by weight it goes rancid faster than wheat flour — off/painty/bitter smell means toss it; store airtight in the fridge (~6 months) or freezer (~1 year).
