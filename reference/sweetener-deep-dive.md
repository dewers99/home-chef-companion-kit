# Sweetener Deep-Dive — Home Chef Companion Kit v1.0.0

**Reference appendix.** Consulted only when the user asks for the evidence behind a sweetener claim. Grades: **A** = consistent human RCT evidence · **B** = human observational (correlation; causation not established) · **C** = animal/in-vitro only · **D** = speculative/mechanistic · **R** = regulatory-consensus position (a policy judgment, not a study).

**Standing caveat:** with one or two exceptions, the long-term human effects of alternative sweeteners are genuinely under-studied. "No credible evidence of harm" is not "proven safe long-term" — and a single observational study is not proof of danger. Where evidence is thin, this document says so.

---

## 1. Allulose (D-psicose)

A "rare sugar" found naturally in tiny amounts in figs, raisins, jackfruit, and wheat. About 70% as sweet as sucrose; bakes and browns much like sugar — which is why keto bakers love it.

- **Glycemic impact — A (short-term):** the FDA's review concluded allulose "negligibly increases glycemic and insulinemic responses" and is virtually unmetabolized (~70% excreted unchanged in urine). A 2024 meta-analysis of 6 RCTs in people with type 2 diabetes (n=126) found allulose significantly reduced postprandial glucose AUC (SMD −0.666, p=0.0054); insulin and fasting glucose unchanged. Reviews consistently add: long-term RCTs are still needed.
- **GI tolerance — A (small trial):** a dose-escalation trial (Han et al., *Nutrients* 2018) found no severe diarrhea up to a single dose of 0.4 g/kg; severe diarrhea appeared at 0.5 g/kg. Suggested limits: max single dose 0.4 g/kg (~27 g for a 150-lb person), max daily 0.9 g/kg. **Funding flag:** two co-authors were affiliated with an allulose producer — don't present these thresholds as independent-agency limits.
- **Regulatory — R, WITH THE VERIFY CORRECTION:** FDA GRAS; the October 2020 final guidance exercises enforcement discretion to **exclude allulose from "Total Sugars" and "Added Sugars"** on U.S. labels (still counted under Total Carbohydrate) and allows **0.4 kcal/g**. **Not authorized in the EU:** EFSA's May 2025 novel-food opinion concluded "the safety of D-allulose cannot be established" because the applicant never replied to EFSA's requests for identity, production, genotoxicity, and human data. That is a **dossier/data gap, not a toxicity finding** — phrase it carefully; don't let anyone read it as "EFSA says it's unsafe."
- **Bottom line:** the strongest short-term glycemic data of the group; essentially no long-term human data.

## 2. Monk fruit extract (mogrosides)

Extract of *Siraitia grosvenorii* (Luo Han Guo); sweet compounds are mogrosides (mainly mogroside V), roughly 150–250× sweeter than sucrose depending on preparation. Zero-calorie at use levels.

- **Glycemic impact — D (thin human data):** mogrosides are poorly absorbed (animal pharmacokinetics — rat data, not human trials). **Retraction flag:** two papers making favorable metabolic claims about monk fruit were **retracted** (a 2025 *Foods*/MDPI review and a mogroside-IVe paper). Their claims must not be cited. No solid published human glycemic RCT could be verified — the thinnest human evidence base of the seven sweeteners.
- **GI tolerance:** no significant complaints in published studies referenced in the GRAS dossiers; use-level doses are tiny.
- **Regulatory — R, WITH THE VERIFY CORRECTION:** FDA GRAS (GRN 301, June 2010, plus later notices). **Not broadly EU-authorized:** EFSA's 2019 opinion found the toxicity database insufficient to conclude on safety as a food additive; only **one specific aqueous extract** got a 2024 positive EFSA opinion. **Critical confusion risk: E 960 is steviol glycosides — not monk fruit.** A secondary market report misattributed an "E 960 approval" to monk fruit; monk fruit has no E number.
- **Bottom line:** low risk signal on a thin evidence base; don't cite metabolic-benefit claims.

## 3. Erythritol

A sugar alcohol (polyol), well tolerated — the easiest on the gut of the polyols.

- **Glycemic impact — A (short-term):** negligible glycemic response at normal doses.
- **Regulatory — R:** FDA GRAS (2001). EFSA's 2023 re-evaluation (E 968) set an **ADI of 0.5 g/kg body weight/day** based on human dose-escalation studies, protective for the laxative effect — and **kept** the *"excessive consumption may produce laxative effects"* warning for foods over 10% polyols.
- **Cardiovascular signal — B (association) + C (mechanism), OBSERVATIONAL/CONTESTED:** Witkowski et al., *Nature Medicine* 2023 — untargeted metabolomics in 1,157 high-CV-risk individuals plus validation cohorts (n=2,149 US; n=833 Europe): higher circulating erythritol was associated with MACE (death/heart attack/stroke) over 3-year follow-up (Q4 vs Q1 HRs 1.80 US, 2.21 Europe, adjusted). Parallel small pilot trials (n=10 each): 30 g erythritol increased platelet responsiveness to ex-vivo stimulation. **Limitations:** no replication in a large independent cohort; critics note the original cohorts did not measure dietary erythritol intake and some samples predate erythritol's food-additive approval — endogenous erythritol synthesis (pentose-phosphate pathway) is a confounder. **Regulatory response: none** — FDA hasn't revised GRAS; EFSA's re-evaluation didn't cover this signal.
- **Bottom line:** present as "observational association, causal link unproven, contested." No scare-tactics, no safety declarations either.

## 4. Xylitol

A sugar alcohol with a low (not zero) glycemic response and dose-dependent laxative effects at higher intakes.

- **Glycemic impact — A/B:** low glycemic response at normal doses.
- **Cardiovascular signal — B (association), OBSERVATIONAL/CONTESTED:** a parallel arm of the erythritol research (Witkowski group) linked higher circulating xylitol to MACE and showed increased platelet responsiveness after 30 g ingestion (n=10 pilot, no placebo arm, single time point). Same replication and confounding caveats as erythritol; regulators unchanged.
- **⚠️ ACUTE DANGER TO DOGS — strong veterinary evidence, VERIFY-CORRECTED AND HARD-CONFIRMED:** Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine: even a small amount triggers rapid, exaggerated insulin release — 3–7× normal — causing hypoglycemia within 30–60 minutes; larger ingestions can cause liver damage or failure. Merck Veterinary Manual: hypoglycemia at **>100 mg/kg**, severe hepatic failure at **>500 mg/kg**; dogs are the only domestic species in which xylitol toxicosis is reported. **Kit rule: never recommend xylitol-sweetened recipes as dog-safe treats; flag "toxic to dogs" on any recipe using xylitol.**
- **Regulatory — R:** FDA GRAS; E 967 (authorized; EFSA re-evaluation still ongoing as of Sept 2026).
- **Bottom line:** uncertain CV signal like erythritol's; the dog toxicity is the clearest, hardest danger in this document.

## 5. Sucralose

Chlorinated sucrose derivative (~600× sweeter than sugar); heat-stable for most cooking, with caveats below.

- **Glycemic impact — A (short-term, with a twist):** Suez et al., *Cell* 2022 — 120 healthy adults randomized to 2 weeks of saccharin, sucralose, aspartame, or stevia (all below the ADI) vs. controls. Each sweetener distinctly altered the microbiome/metabolome; **saccharin and sucralose impaired glycemic responses at the group level**; aspartame and stevia did not. Microbiome transfer to germ-free mice reproduced the donors' glycemic patterns — supporting a microbiome-mediated mechanism. Limitations: 2 weeks; glucose tolerance, not disease, was the endpoint; effects were person-specific. It shows sucralose is not metabolically inert in everyone — not that it causes diabetes.
- **Genotoxicity signal — C (in vitro only, handle with care):** Schiffman et al., 2023 — sucralose-6-acetate (an impurity and a gut metabolite) was genotoxic/clastogenic in validated in-vitro assays on human blood cells and impaired intestinal-barrier integrity in a gut-epithelium model. **Entirely in vitro; unreplicated as of Sept 2026; regulators have not acted.** Never present as "sucralose damages your DNA" without the in-vitro qualifier.
- **Regulatory — R:** FDA approved 1998 (ADI 5 mg/kg/day); JECFA ADI 0–15 mg/kg/day; EFSA's 2025 re-evaluation (E 955) **kept the ADI at 15 mg/kg/day** and found EU exposures below it — **but** flagged an uncertainty it couldn't dismiss: potential chlorine transfer to organic molecules under **prolonged high-temperature conditions** (home baking/frying), and declined a proposed extension of use in fine bakery wares for that reason. Directly relevant to a cooking kit — report the flag, graded.
- **Bottom line:** a genuine human RCT on microbiome-mediated glycemic effects plus an unreplicated in-vitro signal plus a 2025 EFSA high-heat flag, against an unchanged regulatory consensus. Present both, graded.

## 6. Aspartame

Dipeptide methyl ester (~200× sweeter than sugar). Breaks down with prolonged heat — **not suitable for baking** (loses sweetness). Metabolizes to phenylalanine, aspartic acid, and a small amount of methanol.

- **Glycemic impact — A (short-term):** Suez et al., *Cell* 2022 — aspartame did not impair glycemic responses at the group level.
- **The cancer question — B (limited) + R (split agencies):** IARC, July 2023 — **Group 2B, "possibly carcinogenic to humans"** on limited human evidence (three observational liver-cancer studies), limited animal evidence, limited mechanistic evidence. Group 2B is a **hazard** classification (can it cause cancer under some circumstance?), not a **risk** assessment (does it, at real doses?). JECFA (July 2023, third evaluation): "no convincing evidence" of adverse effects; **reaffirmed ADI 0–40 mg/kg/day**. FDA (July 2023): publicly **disagreed** with IARC — "no safety concerns when aspartame is used under the approved conditions" (FDA ADI: 50 mg/kg/day).
- **Phenylketonuria (PKU) — the hard contraindication:** people with PKU cannot metabolize phenylalanine; aspartame products must be labeled as a phenylalanine source (US/EU/UK). Real, labeled, non-controversial — and irrelevant to everyone else at dietary doses.
- **Regulatory — R:** approved in 100+ countries; covered by the WHO 2023 NSS guidance.
- **Bottom line:** the risk-assessment agencies (JECFA, FDA, EFSA) all still say safe within the ADI, which almost nobody reaches. The IARC 2B listing is a hazard flag worth disclosing honestly, not worth alarming a cook over. Genuine risk: PKU.

## 7. Stevia (steviol glycosides)

Extract of *Stevia rebaudiana*; steviol glycosides (stevioside, rebaudiosides A, D, M) are 200–350× sweeter than sugar. Gut bacteria cleave them to steviol, excreted in urine. Heat-stable — fine for baking.

- **Glycemic impact — A (short-term):** Suez et al., *Cell* 2022 — stevia did not impair glycemic responses. JECFA's review: well tolerated with no adverse effects on blood pressure or blood glucose at tested doses.
- **Regulatory — R:** JECFA (2008): ADI **0–4 mg/kg/day** expressed as **steviol equivalents** (100× safety factor on a 2-year rat carcinogenicity study NOAEL); EFSA (2010): same ADI. FDA GRAS via "no questions" letters for **high-purity steviol glycosides** — note the nuance: crude whole-leaf stevia / crude extracts are *not* covered by those determinations. **EU:** approved food additive **E 960** since 2011.
- **Substitution ratios — BRAND-VARIABLE (verify correction):** no regulator or standards body publishes one official sugar-substitution ratio. Literature cites ranges (FSANZ: 200–300×; reviews: 150–450× depending on glycoside — stevioside ~210×, rebaudioside A ~300×, rebaudioside C ~30×). Commercial packets are brand-blended with carriers at brand-chosen dilutions. **The package's own conversion is the only valid ratio** — never hard-code "1 tsp stevia = 1 cup sugar."
- **Bottom line:** the cleanest sheet in this document — decades of toxicology, no human glycemic-impairment signal, no CV signal. Caveats: ADI in steviol equivalents is hard for a cook to track (though intakes rarely approach it); the WHO's conditional NSS guidance technically includes stevia for weight-control purposes.

## 8. Cross-cutting: the WHO 2023 non-sugar sweetener guideline

What the WHO guideline *actually* says (*Use of non-sugar sweeteners: WHO guideline*, Geneva, 2023):

- **The recommendation:** WHO *suggests* (conditional/weak grade) that non-sugar sweeteners **not be used as a means of achieving weight control or reducing the risk of noncommunicable diseases.**
- **Basis:** a commissioned systematic review finding no long-term benefit for body-fat reduction and *possible* undesirable effects with long-term use (higher type 2 diabetes, CVD, mortality). **Certainty: "very low to low"** — precisely why the recommendation is conditional.
- **Scope (commonly misreported):** covers acesulfame K, aspartame, advantame, cyclamates, neotame, saccharin, sucralose, stevia and stevia derivatives — i.e., sucralose, aspartame, stevia, and monk fruit in this document. **Does NOT cover:** sugar alcohols/polyols (erythritol and xylitol are explicitly excluded), personal-care/medical uses, or **people with pre-existing diabetes**. It is about *weight control as a purpose* — not a finding that these substances are toxic.
- **Kit framing:** "The WHO advises against using these sweeteners *for weight loss*, based on low-certainty evidence — it did not declare them unsafe, it excluded diabetics and sugar alcohols, and regulators' ADIs stand." Anything stronger is misreporting.

## Bottom-line grades

| Sweetener | Glycemic safety | GI tolerance | CV/metabolic signal | Overall risk signal | Strongest caveat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Allulose | A — negligible rise; may blunt co-ingested carbs | A (small industry-co-authored trial) | None found | Low | No long-term human data; **not EU-authorized** (dossier gap, not toxicity) |
| Monk fruit | D — assumed neutral, thin human data | Good at use levels | None studied | Low (thin base) | Retracted papers; **not EU-authorized** (E 960 = stevia, not monk fruit) |
| Erythritol | A — no response | Good (best of the polyols) | B — 2023 MACE association, **unreplicated, confounded** | Uncertain | Endogenous production undermines the headline |
| Stevia | A — no impairment | Good | None found | Low | Brand-variable ratios; WHO conditional guidance includes it |
| Xylitol | A/B — low response | Dose-dependent laxative | B — MACE association, **same caveats** | Uncertain; **acutely toxic to dogs** | Dog toxicity is the clearest danger in this document |
| Sucralose | A — but Cell 2022 showed glycemic impairment via microbiome in some people | A (microbiome shifts in 2 weeks) | None (human) | Uncertain | In-vitro genotoxicity of metabolite (C); EFSA 2025 high-heat baking flag |
| Aspartame | A — no impairment | Good | B — IARC 2B (limited evidence) | Low within ADI | PKU contraindication; not heat-stable |
