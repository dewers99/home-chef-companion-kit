# kitchen-math — Home Chef Companion Kit

**Version:** 1.0.0
**Description:** Grams-first conversions, the three real cups, pan-size and yield-scaling rules, altitude adjustments, and measurement-technique guidance for everyday home cooking.

## House rule: grams first, cups as the friendly fallback

A scale is the most reliable measuring tool in a kitchen. Volumes are fine for everyday
cooking, but for baking — where 20% too much flour can turn a cake into a brick — weight
wins. When this skill answers a conversion, it gives **grams (or milliliters) first** and
cups (labeled with which cup) as the fallback.

**Flour method matters.** The weight anchor is King Arthur Baking's ingredient-weight
chart, which assumes flour is **spooned into the cup and leveled** — not scooped. A
dip-and-sweep scoop can pack 140+ g into the same cup that holds 120 g spooned-and-leveled.
When a recipe just says "1 cup flour," spoon-and-level is the assumption.

### Baking ingredient weights (per 1 US cup, spooned-and-leveled)

| Ingredient | 1 cup = grams | 1 cup = oz |
|---|---|---|
| All-purpose flour | **120 g** | 4¼ oz |
| Bread flour | **120 g** | 4¼ oz |
| Whole wheat flour | **113 g** | 4 oz |
| Granulated sugar | **198 g** | 7 oz |
| Brown sugar, packed | **213 g** | 7½ oz |
| Powdered/confectioners' sugar | **113 g** | 4 oz |
| Butter | **227 g** (2 sticks) | 8 oz |
| Cocoa powder, unsweetened | **84 g** | 3 oz |
| Chocolate chips | **170 g** | 6 oz |
| Rolled oats | **90 g** (range 80–99 by source) | ~3½ oz |
| White rice, uncooked | **190 g** | ~6¾ oz |
| Honey | **336 g** (1 Tbsp ≈ 21 g) | 12 oz |
| Vegetable oil | **~200–224 g** | ~7–8 fl oz |
| Table salt | **288 g** (1 tsp ≈ 6 g) | ~10 oz |
| Baking powder | **4 g per tsp** | — |
| Baking soda | **6 g per tsp** | — |
| Large egg, out of shell | **≈50 g** (white ≈ 35 g, yolk ≈ 14 g) | ~1¾ oz |

Brown sugar is measured **packed** unless the recipe says otherwise. Powdered sugar is
usually spooned light (don't pack it).

## The THREE real cups

There is no single "cup." Three different official cups exist, and mixing them up is the
#1 cross-border cooking failure. Know which one a recipe means.

| Cup | Volume | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **US customary cup** | **236.6 mL** (8 fl oz) | NIST definition — this is what US recipes mean by "cup" |
| **FDA label cup** | **240 mL** | US nutrition labeling only (21 CFR 101.9) — *not* a measuring-cup rule |
| **Metric cup** | **250 mL** | Australian standardised metric cup (Standards Australia AS 1325); also standard in NZ |

The spread from 236.6 to 250 mL is about **6%** — invisible in a pot of soup, potentially
real in a small batch of fudge or a bread formula. For baking, weigh.

> **Never say "NIST practical cup."** No such thing exists. The 250 mL cup is the
> Australian metric cup — attributing it to NIST is wrong.

### Cup-subdivision ladder (US customary)

1 cup = **16 Tbsp** = **48 tsp** = 8 fl oz
- ¼ cup = 4 Tbsp · ⅓ cup = 5 Tbsp + 1 tsp · ½ cup = 8 Tbsp
- ⅔ cup = 10 Tbsp + 2 tsp · ¾ cup = 12 Tbsp
- 1 Tbsp = 3 tsp = ½ fl oz ≈ 14.8 mL · 1 tsp ≈ 4.9 mL

**Butter-stick equivalents:** 1 stick = ½ cup = 8 Tbsp = 4 oz by weight = **113 g**.
Half stick = ¼ cup = 4 tbsp = 2 oz = 57 g. Butter wrappers are printed with tablespoon
marks — the stick is its own measuring tool.

## The two collision traps

### The US/imperial pint trap

"One pint" is not one pint. A **US pint** is 16 fl oz (**473 mL**); an **imperial pint**
(UK) is 20 fl oz (**568 mL**) — about **20% larger**. Any time a recipe says "pint,"
check which side of the Atlantic it comes from. (Imperial gallons differ the same way:
4.55 L vs. US 3.79 L.)

### The Australian tablespoon trap

An **Australian tablespoon is 20 mL** — a full 33% more than the US tablespoon (~15 mL).
An Australian recipe's "1 tablespoon of baking powder" measured with a US tablespoon
will under-leaven noticeably; a US recipe's tablespoon of salt measured with a 20 mL
Australian spoon will over-salt. When using a recipe from Australia, check the spoons.
And check the cups too: an Australian recipe's "1 cup" is the 250 mL metric cup —
about 6% more than a US 236.6 mL cup. If you measure an Australian recipe's cups
with a US cup, either weigh the ingredients or scale the cups up by ~6%.

## Liquid vs. dry measuring — use the right cup

They measure the same volume, but they only measure it *reliably* for their own kind:

- **Dry ingredients → dry (nesting) cups.** Overfill slightly, then level with a straight
  edge (back of a knife). Spoon flour in — never scoop from the bag — then level.
  (Brown sugar is the exception: pack it firmly. Ditto packed shortenings.)
- **Liquids → transparent graduated cups.** Set the cup on a flat surface, pour, and read
  at **eye level** from the **bottom of the meniscus** (the curve the liquid makes against
  the wall).

Why it matters: flour measured in a liquid cup comes out heavy and can't be leveled —
across three cups that error can compound to roughly half a cup of flour.

## Cans: ounces are NET WEIGHT, never a unit conversion

A can marked "14.5 oz" contains 14.5 ounces of **net weight**, not 14.5 fluid ounces.
Mapping can ounces to cups as if "8 oz = 1 cup" is **wrong in principle** — the cup
yield depends on the food's density, how much is liquid vs. solids, and whether it's
drained. So instead of a conversion, use these **product-specific approximate yields**
(they're rules of thumb, not math):

| Common can | Net weight | ≈ Yields |
|---|---|---|
| Small (e.g., tomato paste) | ~6 oz | ≈ ¾ cup |
| Standard can | ~8 oz | **≈ 1 cup** |
| No. 300 (typical tomato/bean can) | ~14–16 oz | **≈ 1¾ cups** |
| No. 2½ (large tomato can) | ~28–29 oz | **≈ 3½ cups** |
| #10 (food-service size) | ~104–117 oz | ≈ 13 cups |

Always say **"about"** — drained vs. undrained and product style change the actual
yield. A 16 oz can of diced tomatoes pours to about 2 cups *with* juice but only about
1 cup of actual tomato drained. If the recipe is sensitive, drain and measure (or weigh)
the solids separately.

## Eggs

USDA grades eggs by **weight per dozen** — Large means a minimum of **24 oz per dozen**
(in shell), not a guarantee that any single egg weighs exactly 2 oz.

- **Large egg ≈ 50 g out of shell** (white ≈ 35 g, yolk ≈ 14 g). When a US recipe says
  "1 egg" with no size, it means **large** (convention among recipe developers).
- **Volume:** a large egg is **about 3.25 Tbsp** — an approximate figure that varies
  egg to egg, not a standard.
- Swapping sizes: for 1–2 eggs, any size works. For 3+ eggs (custards, cakes), match by
  weight or whisk and measure by volume. Roughly: 5 medium ≈ 4 large.

## Pinch, dash, and friends — no conversion exists

"Pinch" and "dash" are seasoning **gestures**, not units. No NIST, FDA, USDA, or
Extension authority defines them — so this skill **never encodes a fixed conversion**
like "dash = ⅛ tsp." When a recipe says "a pinch of salt," that means *season to taste*,
not a number you can plug into scaling math. (Cocktail bitters are the one quasi-formal
use, and even there it's by convention, not law.)

## Temperature conversions

Formulas: **°C = (°F − 32) ÷ 1.8** · **°F = (°C × 1.8) + 32**

Handy landmarks:

| °F | °C (conventional) | Gas Mark | Useful for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 225 | 110 | ¼ | Low-and-slow, drying |
| 300 | 150 | 2 | Slow bakes |
| **325** | **160** | **3** | Moderately slow — cheesecakes, custards |
| 350 | 180 | 4 | The all-purpose baking temp |
| 375 | 190 | 5 | Pies, breads |
| 400–425 | 200–220 | 6–7 | Roasting, puff pastry |
| 450+ | 230+ | 8+ | Pizza, searing |

**Gas Mark 3 = 325°F = 160°C.** Some UK charts print 170°C for Gas Mark 3 — that's
wrong (325°F converts to 162.8°C exactly; 160 is the correct chart rounding).

**Convection (fan) ovens:** reduce the conventional temperature by about **20–25°F**
(10–15°C), or start checking doneness ~25% earlier — but don't do both at full strength
on the first try or you'll undercook. When a recipe already gives a convection setting,
follow it as written. (See the cooking-methods skill for when convection is and isn't
the right call.)

## Pan-size swaps

Compare pans by **area** (round = πr²; rectangle = length × width) or by their **cup
capacity**. And never fill any pan more than **½ to ⅔ full** with batter.

| Pan | ≈ Capacity |
|---|---|
| 8″ round | 6 cups |
| 9″ round | 8 cups |
| 9×9×2″ square | 10 cups |
| 9×13″ rectangle | 14 cups |
| 9×5×3″ loaf | 8 cups |
| 10″ Bundt | 12 cups |

Handy equivalences (a 9×13" is the workhorse):

- **9×13″ ≈ two 9″ rounds** ≈ two 8″ squares ≈ one 10″ Bundt
- 9″ round ≈ 8×8×2″ square ≈ 9×5″ loaf
- A same-size square holds ~25% more than a round (9″ square = 81 sq in vs. 9″ round ≈ 64 sq in)

**When you swap pans: keep the oven temperature the same and adjust the time.**
Thinner batter layer → start checking ~5–10 minutes early; deeper batter → later. Only
drop the temp (~25°F) if the edges start over-browning during an extended bake.

## Scaling a recipe up or down

**Scales linearly (just multiply):** flour, sugar, fats, liquids, produce, meat.

**Needs judgment (scale sub-linearly):**

- **Leaveners (baking powder, soda, yeast):** use about **75% of linear** when doubling.
  Full-doubling leaveners tastes metallic and causes rise-then-collapse.
- **Salt, spices, dried herbs, extracts:** start at **60–75% of linear** when doubling,
  then taste and adjust at the end. Heat (cayenne, hot sauce) doubles with doubling —
  season to taste regardless.
- **Eggs:** round to whole eggs; for odd halves, whisk one egg and weigh out what you
  need (1 large egg ≈ 50 g).

**Time and temperature NEVER scale linearly.** Doubling a recipe does not double the
oven temperature — ever. For much larger masses, raise the temp only ~25°F *if* needed
(it usually isn't); pan depth drives bake time more than total quantity. On the stovetop,
doubled batches in the same pan lose proportionally less moisture to evaporation and cook
slower — use a wider pan or cook in batches, and expect **25–50% longer** cook times.
Cooking fat for the pan scales with pan *area*, not the recipe factor. And "to taste"
ingredients don't scale at all — leave them alone.

## High-altitude adjustments

"High altitude" for cooking means roughly **3,000 ft and above**. Two physics changes
drive everything: water boils cooler, and leavening gases expand faster.

### Boiling points (approximate — weather shifts these a few tenths of a degree)

| Elevation | Water boils at about |
|---|---|
| Sea level | 212°F |
| 2,000 ft | 208°F |
| 5,000 ft | **about 202–203°F** |
| 7,500 ft | **about 198°F** |
| 10,000 ft | about 193–194°F |

Rule of thumb: about **1°F lower per 500 ft**. Boiled and simmered foods cook cooler, so
they need more time — and turning up the burner does nothing (water can't get hotter
than its own boiling point). Simmered meats/poultry can take **up to 25% longer** at
5,000 ft. Oven roasting needs **no adjustment** — oven air is oven air.

### Cake/baking adjustments (per CSU and NMSU Extension)

Decrease leavening and sugar, increase liquid and oven temp — the hotter oven sets the
batter before over-expanded gas cells collapse it.

| Adjustment per recipe amount | 3,500–6,500 ft | 6,500–8,500 ft | 8,500–10,000 ft |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leavening — decrease per tsp | ⅛ tsp | ⅛–¼ tsp | ¼ tsp |
| Sugar — decrease per cup | 0–1 Tbsp | 0–2 Tbsp | 1–3 Tbsp |
| Liquid — increase per cup | 1–2 Tbsp | 2–4 Tbsp | 3–4 Tbsp |
| Oven temp — increase | 15–25°F | 15–25°F | 15–25°F |

(Also: NMSU's version of the same table adds ~1 Tbsp flour per cup at altitude and notes
5–8 fewer minutes of bake time per 30 minutes. Both sources agree on direction; amounts
are starting points, not law. Start with small changes and adjust up — or for cake mixes,
just follow the high-altitude directions printed on the box.)

### Candy and deep-frying at altitude

- **Candy: subtract 2°F from the finish temperature per 1,000 ft** (same thing as: reduce
  by the difference between your local boiling point and 212°F). At 5,000 ft, a sea-level
  fudge finish of 238°F becomes **228°F**. Best practice: boil plain water, note *your*
  actual boiling point, and subtract the real difference — that self-calibrates for both
  altitude and weather.
- **Deep-frying: lower the frying temperature about 3°F per 1,000 ft** — moisture
  escapes faster at altitude, so sea-level temps dry the food out before the inside cooks.
  (Doughnuts fried at 370°F at sea level go at ~350–360°F at 5,000 ft.)

### Slow cookers and pressure cookers at altitude

- **Slow cooker:** a genuine safety watch-point. **Thaw food first**, don't lift the lid
  (it costs ~20+ minutes of heat recovery), and — this is the altitude-relevant one —
  **start on HIGH for the first hour** before switching to low. Per USDA FSIS, that's
  **recommended, not mandatory** ("if possible") — cooking on low the whole time is still
  safe. Never reheat or cool food in the cooker.
- **Pressure cooker:** increase cooking time **+5% per 1,000 ft above 2,000 ft**
  (e.g., 20 min at sea level → 23 min at Denver altitude). Labeled **community-tested,
  not extension-endorsed** — it comes from cooker-enthusiast testing, not a USDA or
  university publication, but the physics checks out. (Note: a stovetop weighted gauge
  runs a few degrees cooler at altitude than at sea level; if your cooker has a higher
  weight setting, that's the fix. Always follow the manufacturer's own altitude
  directions first.)
