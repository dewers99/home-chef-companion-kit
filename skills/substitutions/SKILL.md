# substitutions — Home Chef Companion Kit

**Version:** 1.0.0
**Description:** Dairy-free, gluten-free, and egg swaps; DIY pantry fallbacks; keto-sweetener baking behavior with honest evidence grades; neutral postures on contested ingredients; soy-free disambiguation.

---

## Scope and posture

This skill is about **substitution knowledge** — ratios, behavior in the pan or oven, and
what changes. It is not a health lecture, and it never prescribes a diet. The companion
adapts cooking to how the user *already* eats. When a user asks about a contested
ingredient, the stance is **attribute, don't adjudicate**: say what the evidence shows
and what it doesn't, and leave the choice to the user.

## Dairy-free swaps

### Milk → 1 cup
Use an equal volume of unsweetened plant milk, 1:1. Notes:
- **Soy milk** — closest all-around baking sub (neutral, highest protein).
- **Almond milk** — slightly nutty, lower protein; fine in most things.
- **Oat milk** — creamy, mildly sweet; check it's certified GF if gluten matters.
- **Coconut milk** — distinct coconut flavor; best where that fits (curries, some cakes).
- **Rice/cashew milk** — thin but allergy-friendly / mild and creamy.

### Buttermilk (1 cup)
1 Tbsp lemon juice **or** white vinegar + enough milk (dairy *or* non-dairy) to make
1 cup; rest 5–10 minutes until it thickens slightly. Same trick works with plant milk.

### Butter → 1 cup
- **Vegan butter / dairy-free margarine, 1:1** — closest in baking behavior. If the
  recipe called for unsalted butter, reduce recipe salt by ~½ tsp.
- **Coconut oil, 1:1** — works, adds coconut notes.
- **Neutral oil, ~⅞ cup per cup of butter** — oil lacks butter's water, so use a touch
  less. Olive oil works too, but its flavor shows up in the final product.

### Heavy cream → 1 cup (not for whipping unless noted)
- **Coconut cream, 1:1** — richest option; chill a can of full-fat coconut milk and use
  the solid cream. Will *not* reliably whip without technique. Strong coconut flavor.
- **⅔ cup plant milk + ⅓ cup neutral oil** — casseroles, soups, pasta sauces; **will not
  whip**.
- **Blended silken tofu (~8 oz) + ½ cup plant milk ≈ 1 cup** — sauces and soups; will
  not whip.
- **Cashew cream (1 cup cashews blended with ¾–1 cup water)** — neutral, great in
  savory sauces.
- For actual whipped topping: **aquafaba** (whips to stiff peaks) or chilled coconut
  cream, or a store-bought plant-based whipping cream — check its allergens.

### Cream cheese → 8 oz / Sour cream → 1 cup / Yogurt → 1 cup
- Cream cheese: 8 oz **vegan cream cheese, 1:1** (or 8 oz silken tofu blended smooth,
  or Neufchâtel for a dairy 1:1).
- Sour cream: **1 cup plain yogurt (dairy or plant), 1:1.** Heat caveat: yogurt and sour
  cream can curdle at high heat — temper it or add at the end of cooking.
- Yogurt: 1:1 sour cream or buttermilk; dairy-free yogurt 1:1 in cream applications.

## Gluten-free swaps

### 1:1 flour blends
Commercial cup-for-cup GF blends replace wheat flour **1:1 by volume** in most recipes
(King Arthur Measure for Measure already contains xanthan gum — no extra needed).
**Always check the bag first** — blends and boxed mixes that already include xanthan
gum will turn gummy if you double-dose.

### Single GF flours
- **Almond flour:** 1:1 with wheat flour in most recipes (don't pack the measuring cup).
  Add binders (egg, xanthan gum, psyllium, flax), reduce other fat slightly (almond
  flour brings its own), and check the oven early — it browns faster.
- **Coconut flour: ¼–⅓ cup per 1 cup wheat flour.** Extremely absorbent — increase
  liquid or eggs significantly or the result will be dry.
- **Cassava flour:** near 1:1 in non-yeasted recipes (tortillas, brownies, cookies);
  neutral flavor, slight stretch.
- **Oat flour caution:** oat flour and oat milk are **not reliably gluten-free** unless
  certified GF (cross-contact during processing).

### Xanthan gum
**Starting point: ¼ tsp per 1 cup of flour** when your flour/blend does NOT already
contain it. Scale up for elastic doughs (up to ~1 tsp per cup for yeast breads), scale
down for shortbread and cookies that need less structure. Psyllium husk is the common
xanthan alternative in yeast breads.

## Egg swaps (per 1 egg)

| Substitute | Per-egg ratio | Best for | Caveats |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Aquafaba** (chickpea liquid) | **3 Tbsp = 1 whole egg; 2 Tbsp = 1 egg white** | The only sub that **whips to meringue**; meringues, mayo, even brioche | Little structure; batter may smell "bean-y" (bakes out) |
| Flax egg | 1 Tbsp ground flax + 3 Tbsp water, rest 5–10 min until gelled | Muffins, pancakes, quick breads, cookies — binding | Adds binding, not structure — poor for light fluffy cakes; subtle earthy flavor |
| Chia egg | 1 Tbsp chia + 3 Tbsp water, rest ~10–15 min | Same as flax | Same limits as flax |
| Unsweetened applesauce | 3–4 Tbsp per egg | Cakes, quick breads, brownies, muffins — moisture | Denser crumb; adds slight sweetness/apple note |
| Mashed banana | ¼ cup (one small/medium) per egg | Pancakes, muffins, quick breads, brownies | Noticeable banana flavor; denser texture |
| Silken tofu, blended smooth | ¼ cup per egg | Dense bakes: brownies, cheesecakes, custards | Heavy — not for light cakes |
| Plain yogurt / buttermilk | ¼ cup per egg | Cakes, muffins (adds lift + moisture) | Adds tang |

When a sub goes in *for structure* (angel-food cake, meringue), nothing beats aquafaba;
everything else trades structure for moisture and binding. Judge the swap by the job the
egg was doing.

## DIY pantry fallbacks

| Make this | From this | Ratio |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-rising flour** (1 cup) | AP flour + baking powder + salt | 1 cup AP + 1½ tsp baking powder + ½ tsp salt. Already-salted — reduce/skip added salt in the recipe. |
| **Buttermilk** (1 cup) | Milk + acid | 1 Tbsp lemon juice or white vinegar + enough milk to = 1 cup; rest 5–10 min |
| **Brown sugar** (1 cup packed) | White sugar + molasses | 1 cup sugar + 1–2 Tbsp molasses (more for dark); pulse in a food processor |
| **Cake flour** (1 cup) | AP flour + cornstarch | Measure 1 cup AP, remove 2 Tbsp, add 2 Tbsp cornstarch; sift |
| **Powdered sugar** (1 cup) | Granulated sugar | Blend/pulverize in a blender or food processor |
| **Heavy cream** (1 cup, *not* for whipping) | Milk + butter | ¾ cup whole milk + ¼ cup melted butter, cooled; whisk. **Will not whip.** |
| Salted ↔ unsalted butter | Swap either direction | Salted for unsalted: reduce added salt ~¼ tsp per stick |

### Leaveners, thickeners, acids (quick)
- **1 tsp baking powder** = ¼ tsp baking soda + ½ tsp cream of tartar (bake immediately).
- **1 Tbsp cornstarch (thickening)** = 2 Tbsp all-purpose flour. Flour needs a roux or
  slurry and a few minutes of simmering to lose the raw taste.
- Arrowroot = cornstarch 1:1, but it **breaks down under prolonged boiling** (better for
  clear, glossy, acidic or frozen sauces; worse for long simmers).
- **1 tsp lemon juice ≈ ½ tsp white vinegar** in acid-power terms. White distilled or
  cider for neutral baking use; balsamic and red wine bring their own flavor.
- 1 Tbsp fresh herbs ≈ 1 tsp dried (dried are more concentrated; add dried earlier,
  fresh at the end).

## Keto sweetener behavior (substitution knowledge, not a lecture)

These are about **how sweeteners behave in the pan** — the health side gets one-line
honest evidence grades, never hype and never scare tactics. The companion does not
declare sweeteners "good" or "bad"; it says what they do in food and what the evidence
actually shows.

### Ratios first — check the label, always

| Sweetener | Sweetness vs. sugar | Replace 1 cup sugar with |
|---|---|---|
| Allulose, pure/granular | ~70% | **1⅓ cups** |
| Erythritol, pure/granular | ~60–70% | **1¼–1⅓ cups** |
| Monk fruit **blends** (most grocery "monk fruit" is monk fruit extract + erythritol or allulose) | Formulated to 100% | **1:1** |
| Pure monk fruit extract | 150–250× | A tiny fraction — **follow the package**, not a table |
| Pure stevia extract | 150–450× (varies by glycoside) | **Follow the package** — no official ratio exists; brand-chosen dilutions make every chart misleading |
| Xylitol | ~100% | 1:1 **⚠️ toxic to dogs — see warning below** |

**"Monk fruit sweetener" on a package is not a ratio.** Always check the label for the
bulking agent — the behavior follows the bulking agent, not the monk fruit.

### Behavior notes

- **Allulose browns and caramelizes FASTER than sugar.** Drop the oven temp **25°F** or
  check about 5 minutes early. It also yields softer, puffier baked goods and doesn't
  crisp like sugar. (This is the #1 allulose baking behavior — the thing that ruins
  cookies when nobody warns about it.) Allulose does *not* recrystallize — it's the
  preferred pick for ice cream, frozen desserts, and cold sauces (smooth, scoopable).
- **Erythritol recrystallizes on cooling** — gritty frostings, glazes, and refrigerated
  desserts. Use **powdered erythritol** for frostings and glazes; serve fresh; a blend
  with allulose disrupts the crystal lattice and smooths texture. Erythritol also has a
  noticeable menthol-like "cooling" mouthfeel at high concentrations (powdered form and
  warm spices like cinnamon mask it), and it does **not** brown.
- **Stevia** has no bulk and a bitter/metallic edge at high doses — it works best as a
  blending agent, not a solo baking sweetener.
- **Xylitol** swaps 1:1 and bakes much like sugar — with the dog-safety rule below.
- **Aspartame breaks down with prolonged heat** — not suitable for baking; coffee and
  cold drinks only.

### One-line evidence grades (behavior-relevant, honest)

- **Allulose** — best short-term glycemic data of the group (FDA: negligibly raises
  blood sugar; excluded from Total/Added Sugars on US labels; 0.4 kcal/g). Long-term
  human data essentially absent — not "proven safe forever," just no credible harm
  signal at normal culinary doses. **Not EU-authorized**: EFSA's May 2025 novel-food
  opinion closed the dossier *without a safety finding* because the applicant stopped
  answering its questions — a **dossier gap, not a toxicity finding**. FDA GRAS stands.
  EU cooks need a different sweetener.
- **Erythritol** — essentially no blood-sugar response; among the best-tolerated
  polyols. A 2023 *Nature Medicine* study linked higher blood erythritol to
  cardiovascular events — **an observational association, contested and unreplicated,
  not proof of danger** (the study couldn't control for dietary intake, and the body
  itself makes erythritol from glucose). Regulators haven't changed any status.
- **Xylitol** — low glycemic impact, established dental benefit, and the same
  observational-CV-signal caveat as erythritol's. The human story is genuinely mixed;
  the dog story is not. **See the warning below.**
- **Monk fruit** — FDA GRAS, but the human evidence base is thin: **"probably
  glycemic-neutral," never "proven beneficial"** (favorable metabolic claims trace
  partly to retracted papers). **Not EU-authorized** — only one specific aqueous
  extract has a positive 2024 EFSA opinion. And: **E 960 is stevia, not monk fruit** —
  don't let anyone conflate them.
- **Stevia** — the cleanest regulatory and evidence sheet (EU-authorized since 2011,
  FDA GRAS, decades of toxicology, no glycemic-impairment signal). Caveat: ratios are
  brand-variable — use the label.
- **Sucralose** — FDA-approved since 1998 with unchanged ADIs, but **real open
  questions**: one solid human RCT (Suez et al., *Cell* 2022) showed microbiome-mediated
  glycemic impairment in some people, and EFSA's 2025 re-evaluation kept the ADI while
  flagging **unresolved uncertainty about chlorine transfer under prolonged
  high-temperature conditions — i.e., home baking specifically**. Regulators and
  researchers point in different directions here; report both.
- **Aspartame** — IARC's 2023 **hazard flag** ("possibly carcinogenic," Group 2B, on
  *limited* evidence) sits alongside **unchanged ADIs from JECFA, FDA, and EFSA**,
  which all did risk assessments (how much harm at real doses) and kept their numbers.
  Present the split, not one side. Two facts beyond dispute: it's not heat-stable for
  baking, and **people with PKU must avoid it** (it's a phenylalanine source — that's
  why the label warns).

> **WHO 2023 non-sugar-sweetener guideline, in one sentence:** the WHO *suggests* these
> sweeteners not be used *for weight control* — a conditional (weak) recommendation on
> low-certainty evidence, which did **not** declare them unsafe, excluded sugar
> alcohols, and excluded people with diabetes. Report that accurately; anything stronger
> is misreporting.

### 🐕 XYLITOL — TOXIC TO DOGS (household safety rule)

**The companion must attach this warning to every recipe using xylitol.**

In dogs, xylitol triggers a massive insulin release (3–7× normal), causing rapid,
profound low blood sugar — **hypoglycemia over ~100 mg/kg body weight, within 30–60
minutes of ingestion** — and larger doses (over ~500 mg/kg) can cause **acute liver
failure**, which can be fatal. Even a single stick of sugar-free gum can poison a
small dog. Sources: Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine, Merck
Veterinary Manual.

- **Never** recommend xylitol-sweetened recipes as dog-safe treats.
- Xylitol is increasingly sold under alternate names — **birch sugar, E 967** — teach
  all of them, since the renaming exists precisely because consumers learned "xylitol"
  kills dogs.
- Any suspected dog ingestion is a **veterinary emergency** — do not "wait and see."

## Neutral postures on contested ingredients

### Seed oils (canola, soybean, corn, sunflower, etc.)

The largest available datasets contradict the "seed oils are toxic" framing. Rather than
adjudicating internet arguments, this skill gives cooks the *practical* guidance:

- **Smoke points (approximate — refined oils run higher than unrefined):**

| Oil | ≈ Smoke point | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Avocado, refined | ~520°F | High-heat searing, stir-frying |
| Peanut, refined | ~450°F | Deep-frying |
| Canola/rapeseed, refined | ~400–450°F | General high-heat cooking |
| Vegetable/soybean blend | ~400–450°F | General high-heat cooking |
| Olive oil, extra-virgin | ~350–410°F | Sautéing, finishing (not deep-frying) |
| Coconut, unrefined | ~350°F | Medium-heat cooking |

- **Don't reuse frying oil.** Once oil darkens, foams, or smokes below its usual point,
  it's spent — strain it into the trash (not the drain) and start fresh. Repeated
  high-heat reuse is where the genuine chemistry concerns live.
- If a user prefers to avoid seed oils entirely, support it without arguing — suggest
  avocado oil, olive oil, butter, or ghee for their preferred tasks and move on.

### Soy

Soy fears (hormones, thyroid, "soy boys") are largely debunked by the research, but the
skill's job isn't to win that argument: if a user wants to avoid soy, support the
avoidance without arguing, and give them working alternatives. If they don't, soy is
fine as an ingredient. Never moralize either choice.

### Nutritional yeast

- **B12 comes only from fortification.** Yeast doesn't make B12 — if the label lists
  B12 (or cyanocobalamin), it was added. Unfortified brands provide **none**. If
  someone's counting on nooch for B12, check the label.
- **Niacin load:** fortified brands are heavily fortified — one brand's listing shows
  **~35 mg niacin per 2 tbsp** (another brand ~25 mg). The niacin UL for adults is
  35 mg/day from supplements and fortified foods. This isn't alarm — it's a note for
  **heavy daily users** (multiple big spoonfuls) to check their own package, because
  the numbers were read from retailer label listings, not the brand's own page, and
  brands reformulate. Niacin UL counts *supplements + fortified foods*, not food-bound
  niacin.

### Psyllium husk

Psyllium is a fiber, but its label (a real FDA-approved health claim exists) carries
**medication-timing instructions** — it can affect how meds are absorbed. For any
question about *when* to take it or how it interacts with a medication, **refer the
user to their pharmacist** (the label itself is the basis; this is a DailyMed
FDA-label matter, not something a cooking companion freelances).

### Almond flour

Dense: **600+ kcal per cup**, and its oils go rancid fast — **fridge or freezer** for
storage, not the pantry shelf. Also the browning note: check the oven early, as above.

## Soy-free warnings — name products precisely

**Tamari and liquid aminos are NOT soy-free.** Both are made from soybeans. Only
**coconut aminos** is soy-free. This distinction matters because "gluten-free" and
"soy-free" are routinely confused:

- **Bragg sells BOTH** — "Bragg Liquid Aminos" (regular) is made **from soybeans and
  water** (NOT soy-free), while "Bragg Coconut Liquid Aminos" is coconut blossom
  nectar + water + apple cider vinegar + salt (**soy-free**). Always name them by full
  name.
- **Kikkoman Tamari (gluten-free)** — ingredients: water, **soybeans**, salt, spirit
  vinegar. **Gluten-free ≠ soy-free.** It's a soy sauce variant; soy-allergic users
  must not use it.
- **Coconut Secret Coconut Aminos** — coconut tree sap + sea salt; marketed and
  verified as **soy-free**.

For a soy-free substitute for soy sauce/tamari/liquid aminos: coconut aminos, 1:1
(slightly sweeter, slightly less salty — adjust to taste).

## When to reach for this skill

- "What can I use instead of eggs / milk / butter / flour?" → the swap tables
- "I'm baking with allulose and my cookies keep burning." → allulose behavior note
- "Is erythritol dangerous?" → one-line evidence grade + behavior note, no scare tactics
- "Can my dog have this?" when xylitol is involved → **dog warning, every time**
- "I want to avoid soy — what can I use?" → coconut aminos, support without arguing
- "I'm out of ___" mid-recipe → DIY pantry fallbacks
- "What sweetener should I use for frosting?" → powdered erythritol or allulose, with the recrystallization caveat
