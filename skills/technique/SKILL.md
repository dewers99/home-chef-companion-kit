## PART 1 — BROTHS & STOCKS

### The honest taxonomy

The classical distinction is short, from the bones-up:

| Type | Made from | Simmer time | Signature trait |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stock** | Bones (+ some meat) | 4–8+ hours chicken; 8–20 beef/veal | **Gels when chilled** — that's the gelatin |
| **Broth** | Meat (with or without some bone) | 1–2 hours | Meaty flavor, stays fluid when chilled |
| **"Bone broth"** | Bones, long-simmered | 12–48 hours | **A long-simmered stock with a marketing name** — no separate classical definition |
| **Vegetable stock** | Vegetables + aromatics | 45 min–2 hours | No gelatin; light (don't overcook cruciferous veg — bitterness) |
| **Fish stock / fumet** | Bones/heads of **lean white fish** only | **~30 minutes max** | Delicate; long simmering ruins it |
| **Dashi** | Kombu + katsuobushi (or niboshi, or shiitake) | Minutes — a steep, not a simmer | Clear, intensely savory; never boiled |
| **Mushroom stock** | Browned fresh + dried mushrooms | ~1 hour | The vegetarian "beefy" stand-in |

Classical rule: **stock is made unsalted** (it's an ingredient that will be reduced —
salt concentrates), and **broth is salted after straining** (it's meant to be eaten as
is). This is the highest-leverage seasoning lesson in the topic.

### The jiggle test

Chill your stock overnight. **Does it gel?** Then you made stock. If it stays pourable,
you effectively made broth — nothing wrong with that, but it tells you about body. On a
grocery label, "stock" and "broth" are **legally interchangeable** (USDA's labeling
policy book: no distinction between the two) — so a carton labeled "stock" is almost
always broth-thin. If you need real stock body from the store, buy unsalted broth and
add gelatin, or make your own.

### When to use which

- **Gelatinous stock → sauces, gravies, braises, reductions.** Gelatin gives silk and
  body — and remember stock is unsalted, so reductions won't turn into salt bombs.
- **Lighter broth → sipping, light soups, and cooking starches.** Tested guidance
  (Serious Eats): a highly gelatinous stock can interfere with starch hydration, so
  risotto, rice, beans, and pasta cook better in broth. *"Pragmatism is law in the
  kitchen":* water down a stock to approximate broth, or whisk unflavored gelatin into
  a broth to approximate stock.
- **Fish stock substitutes for nothing else — and nothing substitutes for it.** Too
  specific a flavor family. In a seafood dish with no fish stock, half clam juice + half
  water is an approximation, not a match.
- **Dashi** is not interchangeable with Western stocks (clean oceanic umami vs. meaty).
  For vegetarians, mushroom/kombu dashi can sub for bonito dashi; Western stock can't
  stand in without changing the dish's identity.

### Dashi rules

Awase (combined) dashi: soak kombu in cold water, heat gently to ~140–158°F
(**remove the kombu before it boils** — boiling releases bitter, slimy compounds),
then add katsuobushi, steep 2–3 minutes off the boil, and strain **without pressing**
the flakes (pressing extracts harsh flavors). Whole active process: ~15–20 minutes.
- Don't wash the white powder off kombu — it's mannitol and glutamate, not mold.
- **Ichiban dashi** (first extraction): clear, delicate — clear soups, chawanmushi.
- **Niban dashi** (second extraction): bolder, less refined — miso soup, simmered dishes.

### Storage (USDA FSIS)

Homemade stock/broth: **3–4 days refrigerated (≤40°F), 2–3 months frozen (0°F)** for
best quality. (Frozen food held constantly at 0°F stays safe indefinitely; the 2–3
month figure is quality.) Cool it fast — don't leave it at room temperature more than
~2 hours. Dashi keeps ~3 days refrigerated. Freeze in ice-cube trays (each cube is
~1–2 Tbsp — perfect for pan sauces), flat zip-top bags, or straight-sided jars with
headspace. Label with contents and date.

### Collagen claims, graded honestly

The mechanism is real cooking chemistry: simmering bones unwinds **collagen** into
**gelatin**, which is why good stock jiggles. What isn't supported:

- **No published human trials test bone broth itself** for joints, skin, or gut —
  only gelatin, hydrolyzed collagen *supplements*, or the amino acids. Bone broth's
  collagen doses run well below supplement doses used in trials.
- Bone broth's typical minerals are modest (a cup has about as much calcium as half a
  cup of broccoli). The "add vinegar to extract minerals" trick is folk wisdom, not
  proven.
- What bone broth honestly offers: a warm, savory, hydrating liquid with modest
  protein, some electrolytes, and gelatin mouthfeel. A real culinary virtue — just not
  medicine, and not a probiotic anything.

**Bottom line the companion teaches:** the jiggle test is a cooking fact; the health
claims for broth itself have no human trials behind them. Never present broth as
medicine.

## PART 2 — YEAST & PROOFING

### The four leavening yeasts

| Yeast | How to use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Active dry (ADY)** | Bloom in warm water **110–115°F** (a pinch of sugar helps) 5–10 min until foamy; then add to the dough | Modern ADY **no longer requires proofing** — improved manufacturing means you can also mix it straight into the dry ingredients like instant (King Arthur's tested guidance). Check the manufacturer's instructions. Keep the bloom step when yeast freshness is in doubt — it's your diagnostic. |
| **Instant (rapid-rise, quick-rise, bread-machine)** | Mix **directly into the flour**, then add liquids at **~120–130°F** | Rises faster than ADY. Instant is sensitive to ice-cold liquids — if the liquid is cold, mix the dough a minute before adding the yeast. |
| **Fresh (cake) yeast** | Crumble into cool liquid **70–80°F** | Best for flavor and activity, per professional bakers; short shelf life (refrigerate, use within ~10 days). |
| **Wild yeast (sourdough starter)** | Feed it, don't buy it | Slow, variable, flavorful. Proofing is measured in hours. See Part 3. |

### Numbers to tattoo on your forearm

- **ADY bloom: 110–115°F.** Instant takes ~120–130°F liquids when blended with flour.
- **Yeast dies at ~140°F.** Never expose yeast to liquids above 140°F.
- **ADY ↔ instant substitutes 1:1, in both directions.** Same amount, just add instant
  with the dry ingredients. Teach 1:1 — no history lesson. (Older references mention
  a 0.75× factor, but no manufacturer sources that explanation; current
  manufacturer guidance is 1:1.)
- 1 packet = ¼ oz = 7 g ≈ 2¼ tsp of active dry.
- Ideal fermentation zone: **75–80°F** dough temperature.

### Proofing: judge the dough, not the clock

Timing doubles roughly every ~15°F of kitchen temperature, so "let rise 1 hour" written
for a 75°F kitchen means ~2 hours at 65°F and ~30 minutes at 85°F.

**The poke test (floured fingertip, press ~½ inch deep):**
- Springs back **immediately and completely** → **under-proofed** — needs more time
  (easiest mistake to fix: just wait, ideally somewhere warmer).
- Springs back **slowly and only partway**, leaving a slight dimple → **properly
  proofed** — bake now.
- Dent **stays put / barely springs back**; dough fragile or deflating → **over-proofed** —
  bake immediately.

Other properly-proofed cues: pillowy, slightly domed, feels light and airy; a few small
bubbles on the surface are fine. In a loaf pan, the crown sits ~1 inch above the rim.

**Over-proofed rescue:** yes, usually, if caught in time. Gently punch down, reshape,
and re-proof — the second proof will be **faster** (yeast is already active), so watch
it closely, and expect a slightly more acidic flavor. If the dough collapses when
touched and has no structure left, re-shaping won't save the rise — repurpose it as
flatbread, pizza, or focaccia, where structure matters less.

**Storage:** unopened dry yeast keeps ~2 years cool and dry; opened ADY goes in an
airtight container in the fridge; opened instant keeps **years** in the freezer.
Bring chilled yeast to room temperature (~30–45 min) before using.

## PART 3 — SOURDOUGH

### The core mental model

A starter is a jar of flour and water hosting wild yeast (makes gas = lift) plus
lactic-acid bacteria (makes acid = tang, flavor, keeping quality). Every technique —
feeding ratios, temperature, timing — is about managing that colony: keeping it fed and
catching it at peak activity.

**The master rule: time is an output, not an input.** Starter readiness, bulk length,
and proof length are all functions of temperature, starter strength, and formula.
Teach reading the dough — volume growth, bubbles, poke test, crumb reading — and treat
every recipe's times as "in a 75°F kitchen" estimates.

### Starter readiness: the float test is debunked

Drop a spoonful of starter in water. If it floats, it contains trapped gas — that's
*all* it tells you. King Arthur's deliberate testing (Sourdough Myths, Myth #9):
fed starter only partway risen **floated anyway**, and a fully ready dense whole-grain
starter **sank**. Floating = has gas, not = ready to bake.

- **If it sinks, it's definitely not ready.** That's the only solid inference.
- **If it floats, it *might* be ready** — confirm with the real test.
- **The real readiness test:** the starter **reliably doubles in size within 6–8 hours
  of feeding**, is very bubbly, and smells pleasantly tangy/yeasty rather than sharp
  or boozy.

### Feeding basics (by weight, always)

Ratios are written **starter : flour : water**, by grams — volume lies (a cup of bubbly
starter weighs much less than a cup of flat starter). Common feeds: 1:1:1 (standard
refresh), 1:2:2 (classic daily feed), 1:5:5 (overnight timing — bigger feed = later
peak, and it dilutes accumulated acid so the flavor stays milder). Room-temp starters
want feeding roughly twice daily; fridge starters get fed weekly and need 1–2 days of
counter feedings to wake up before bake day.

### The basic bake stages (times are warm-kitchen estimates)

1. **Autolyse** (20–60 min, sometimes skipped): flour + water rested before anything
   else hydrates the flour and starts gluten development. Salt and starter go in after.
   Not essential — plenty of good loaves skip it.
2. **Bulk ferment** (3–8 hours warm; 8–14+ cool): the long first rise, starting the
   moment starter meets dough. Stretch-and-folds every ~30–45 minutes for the first
   2–3 hours build strength gently. **Done when the dough has grown ~30–75%** (not
   necessarily doubled), feels airy and jiggly, shows surface bubbles — *volume rise,
   not clock time*.
3. **Pre-shape, bench rest, final shape** (~30–45 min total): gentle handling; surface
   tension in the skin is what gives the loaf lift and oven spring.
4. **Cold proof** (8–24 hours in the fridge; or 30–60 min warm): cold slows the yeast
   while the bacteria keep working — flavor develops, scheduling gets easy, and cold
   firm dough scores cleanly.
5. **Bake** (~40–50 min): very hot with steam — 450–500°F, usually a Dutch oven with
   the lid on for the first ~20–30 min (steam = thin crisp crust, good oven spring),
   lid off to brown. Cool at least **1 hour** before slicing — the crumb is still
   setting and an early slice turns gummy.

### Reading the crumb (the debugging skill)

- **Tall loaf + dense gummy crumb + tunnels/holes** = under-proofed (the #1 beginner
  failure). Fix: longer bulk; judge by volume.
- **Flat loaf + ragged holes + sour smell** = over-proofed. Fix: shorter bulk, watch
  temperature.
- **Under-baked** also goes gummy: loaf feels heavy, bottom sounds dull instead of
  hollow — bake longer, or check internal temp (~205–210°F at sea level; lower at
  altitude, since water boils lower there).

### The label problem

There is **no legal definition of "sourdough" in the US or UK**. Any bread can carry
the name regardless of how it was made — most supermarket "sourdough" is commercial
yeast risen in 1–2 hours with the tang faked by vinegar or citric acid. Real sourdough:
flour, water, salt, a live starter, fermented slowly (~12–48 hours total) with no
commercial yeast. Short ingredient list = good sign.

And one honest note: **baking kills the cultures.** The digestibility and
glycemic-response benefits come from what fermentation *did to the flour* (acids,
partial breakdown) — not from live microbes. There is no "probiotic sourdough"; don't
claim it.

### Temperature and elevation (the fast version)

Fermentation speed roughly **doubles every ~15°F** of dough temperature — warmer is
faster at everything. Below ~65°F it crawls; above ~80°F it races. At altitude
(~3,000 ft+), gas expands more easily so dough rises *faster* (shorter bulk and proof,
or slow it back down with less starter and cooler water), flour is drier, and the
loaf's interior sets at a lower temperature. Everywhere: judge by the dough, not the
clock.
